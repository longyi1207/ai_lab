# Biology, medicine and neuroscience: the bottleneck is measurement, not proof

> Tags: **[established]** textbook-level consensus; **[recent]** results from 2024–2026, which may still be revised; **[speculation]** inference without direct evidence; **⚠** unverified; check it again yourself before citing.

## 20.0 The bottom line

1. **Biology has no "list of conjectures waiting for proof."** Math and physics can list problems like "the Riemann hypothesis" or "Navier–Stokes regularity": clearly stated, missing only a proof (**type A**). Biology can list almost none. The great majority of its hard problems are **type B**: we don't even know which quantities to measure or which equations to write down. So the **direct** contribution of "AI finishing math" to biology is small. This is structural: a biological system evolved over 4 billion years, has no design document, and every one of its parts is an exception to some other part.

2. **But there is one real exception: sampling problems in the sense of statistical mechanics.** Protein dynamics and free energy calculations come down to "computing a partition function over a 10^4–10^6-dimensional space": there are variance bounds, convergence results, things that can be proved. This is the only card in this section where "math is the bottleneck" holds up (Problem 1). Here AI does not work by proving theorems but by **learning a generative model that samples directly from the Boltzmann distribution** [recent].

3. **The second exception is causal identifiability and experimental design.** Behind both the virtual cell (Problem 3) and clinical trials (Problem 5) sits a clean math problem: *given the set of perturbations I can perform, which causal structures are identifiable? What is the most sample-efficient perturbation design?* This sits at the edge of type A. But it is not the actual rate-limiting step. The rate-limiting steps are **not being able to run that many perturbations**, and **evaluation metrics being gamed until they break**.

4. **Bottlenecks ranked by how often they appear: experimental throughput > data with causal design > concepts > institutions > compute > math.** Two pieces of evidence: (a) in 2025 a *Nature Methods* paper tested 7 deep learning models (including 5 single-cell foundation models) on predicting the effects of gene perturbations, and **not one beat a baseline a schoolchild could compute: "add up the log fold changes of the two single perturbations"** [recent]; (b) Arc Institute's first Virtual Cell Challenge drew more than 1,200 teams, and on the MAE component **no model beat the baseline**; the organizers clipped the penalty at 0, so the metric stopped working and was openly gamed [recent]. What's missing is not model capacity but **data and metrics that can push models into a corner**.

5. **"AI's other capabilities" matter an order of magnitude more than "AI's math ability."** Four things: **automated labs** (OpenAI × Ginkgo used GPT-5 to run 36,000 cell-free protein synthesis recipes on 580 automated plates, cutting cost per gram of protein by 40% and raising yield by 27% [recent]); **generative design** (RFdiffusion2 solved all 41 enzyme design benchmarks, where the previous best solved only 16 [recent]); **hypothesis/literature agents** (an epigenetic target recommended by Google's AI co-scientist really was anti-fibrotic in human liver organoids [recent]); **predictive models as "virtual instruments"** (the MICrONS digital twin can predict how more than 10,000 neurons in mouse visual cortex respond to **unseen** stimuli [recent]). None of the four requires proving any theorem.

6. **Medicine's time constants will slow everything down, and this is an institutional problem, not a scientific one.** The overall clinical development success rate is 7.9% (Phase I → approval, 9,704 programs) [established]; the narrowest gate is Phase II→III at 28.9% [established]; 40–50% of failures are due to lack of efficacy [established]. AI-discovered molecules have an 80–90% success rate in Phase I (40–65% for human-discovered ones), but in Phase II they fall back to about 40%, the same as the industry average [recent, small sample]. **This shows precisely that AI is currently solving "is the molecule good?" (chemistry), not "is the target right?" (biology).**

7. **Neuroscience is the field most tightly linked to AI interpretability, and the link is "bad news."** The complete connectome of the nematode *C. elegans* has existed since 1986: 302 neurons, about 7,000 synapses. **Forty years later we still cannot predict its behavior from the connectome** [established]. **If "complete wiring diagram + complete activity recording" still doesn't add up to "understanding," then mechanistic interpretability's goal of "fully understanding a model" needs to be redefined as "making verifiable causal predictions on specific questions."** The good news is that artificial networks are easier than brains: you can read every weight, interventions cost nothing, and you can retrain ten thousand times (Problem 6).

8. **Biosecurity is the only field where "AI capability directly triggers deployment restrictions" has already happened, and its bottleneck is 100% institutional.** US EO 14292 (2025-05) required OSTP to update the 2024 nucleic acid synthesis screening framework within 90 days; as of July 2026 it **still had not been updated** (CRS) [recent]; there is **no federal legislation** for mandatory screening. Meanwhile, in May 2025 Anthropic activated ASL-3 with Claude Opus 4, explicitly because it **could not rule out** CBRN risk [established]. The threat side follows a capability curve that doubles every 129 days; the defense side follows the legislative cycle.

## 20.1 Why these problems

Three dimensions: scale of impact; an honest answer to "is math the bottleneck?"; and tractability for AI. The second dimension does **not** mean picking only cards where "math is the bottleneck." Several cards where "math is not the bottleneck at all" were chosen on purpose (Problems 2, 5, 8), because their answer to "what happens after AI solves math?" is exactly "**not much, because something else is in the way**," and that answer is itself informative. The eight cards form a chain:

> Protein dynamics (Problem 1, physical layer) → de novo design (Problem 2, engineering layer) → predicting cell behavior (Problem 3, systems layer) → how the genome encodes a body (Problem 4, developmental layer) → clinical translation (Problem 5, medical layer). The two side branches are **the brain** (Problem 6, the hardest special case within the systems layer) and **aging** (Problem 7, a phenomenon in which all layers fail together); **biosecurity** (Problem 8) is the shadow side of the capability gains in Problems 1, 2 and 4.

**Not chosen, but worth mentioning:** **Epidemiological modeling**: the math of SEIR has long been good enough; the 2020 misses happened because data on contact matrices, behavior change and testing bias were unavailable, not because the equations were wrong. It is the cleanest example of "math is not the bottleneck," but AI's leverage is small. **Evolution and fitness landscapes**: the same kind of mathematical object as protein energy landscapes and neural network loss landscapes, but the value is mainly epistemic. **Immunology: TCR–antigen recognition and vaccine design**: the practical impact is huge; it was left out only because its bottleneck structure overlaps heavily with Problems 2 and 3. **If you pick one underrated direction, this is the first choice.** **The microbiome**: lots of correlation, very few causal conclusions; intervention experiments in humans are impossible, and community dynamics are not identifiable. **Protein aggregation** (tau, α-synuclein, Aβ) is physically the same as Problem 1; for **agriculture and photosynthesis engineering** the bottleneck is the field-trial cycle (one season per year); for **brain–computer interfaces and whole-brain emulation** the bottleneck is Problem 6's "wiring diagram ≠ algorithm"; for **metabolic engineering** the bottleneck is the experimental throughput of strain engineering.

## 20.2 Problem 1: Protein dynamics and function: the truly hard half after AlphaFold

**In one sentence** Static structure is basically solved, but function comes from the protein's **probability distribution** over a set of conformations and the **free energy differences** between them. Computing this distribution is a high-dimensional sampling problem, and this is the place in all of biology where "math/algorithms really are the bottleneck" stands up best.

**Background** Assign an energy to each 3D configuration of a protein (the sum of electrostatics, hydrogen bonds and the hydrophobic effect) and you get an **energy landscape** with thousands of dimensions; folding is downhill motion on this landscape. **Levinthal's paradox (1969)** [established]: take 100 residues and count only 3 conformations per residue (a huge underestimate); the total number of configurations is 3^100 ≈ 5×10^47. At 10^-13 seconds per step, exhaustive search would take 1.6×10^27 years, 10^17 times the age of the universe; yet real folding takes microseconds to seconds. The resolution is that the landscape is not randomly rugged but **funnel-shaped (folding funnel)**: the closer to the correct structure, the systematically lower the energy. Its mathematical origin is Bryngelson and Wolynes's 1987 derivation from the statistical mechanics of **spin glasses** [established], and the same math was later used to analyze the loss landscapes of neural networks (a Hopfield network is a spin glass). **This is the section's first cross-field link: spin glasses ↔ protein folding ↔ neural network training** (same origin as Mézard–Parisi in §13).

AlphaFold2/3 brought structure prediction to experimental accuracy [established], and Hassabis and Jumper received half of the 2024 Nobel Prize in Chemistry for it (the other half went to Baker for protein design) [established]. But AlphaFold gives **a photograph**, while function comes from **a movie**: **allostery** (a molecule binding at site A changes the shape of site B; many drug targets rely on this mechanism), **cryptic pockets** (invisible in static structures, opening only briefly as the protein "breathes"; many "undruggable" targets actually have them), **intrinsically disordered proteins** (IDPs, about 30% of the human proteome), and **conformational free energy differences**. The mathematical core: the probability of configuration x follows the Boltzmann distribution `p(x) ∝ exp(−E(x)/kT)`; every quantity we want is an expectation under it, and the partition function is a 10^4–10^6-dimensional integral that can only be sampled. `p_A/p_B = exp(ΔG/kT)`, and at room temperature `kT ≈ 0.6` kcal/mol:

| ΔG | Probability ratio | What it means |
|---|---|---|
| 0.6 kcal/mol | ~2.7 : 1 | Both conformations are common |
| 1.4 kcal/mol | ~10 : 1 | One takes 91% |
| 2.8 kcal/mol | ~100 : 1 | One takes 99% |
| 4.2 kcal/mol | ~1000 : 1 | The other is essentially invisible |

So you need **1 kcal/mol accuracy** just to tell "common" from "rare" conformations, and that is about one weak hydrogen bond, a few parts in 10,000 of a protein's total energy. Molecular dynamics (MD) needs a step of about 1 femtosecond, while conformational changes take microseconds to milliseconds, **so you need 10^9–10^12 steps**; simulating 1 millisecond takes **about 5–30 GPU-years** at a few hundred nanoseconds per GPU-day, and longer for bigger systems [speculation, hand estimate at 100–500 ns/day].

**Why it matters** Small-molecule drugs work by binding pockets on proteins. Computing conformational distributions accurately = systematically enumerating cryptic pockets = turning "undruggable" targets into druggable ones (the global prescription drug market is on the order of $1.5 trillion per year). Enzyme catalysis comes from stabilizing the **transition state**; if you can't compute free energies, you can only rely on trial and error (Problem 2).

**What's blocking it: is math the bottleneck?** **Math/algorithms are one of the main bottlenecks, alongside force field accuracy and compute, and the latter two are closing in. This is the only card in this section that is willing to say "math really is the bottleneck."** Sampling is math/algorithms (replica exchange, Markov state models and Boltzmann generators each brought order-of-magnitude speedups); force field accuracy is physical modeling + data (classical force fields have systematic errors; quantum chemistry is accurate but 10^6 times more expensive); compute has a hard wall of 10^12 steps; validation data is an experimental bottleneck (NMR, multi-conformation cryo-EM, HDX-MS and single-molecule FRET are scarce and indirect).

**What AI can do** **(a) Math/proof ability: not much, but not zero.** Things that can be formalized: lower bounds on the effective sample size of importance sampling under a learned proposal distribution (type A: clearly stated, missing a proof); formal verification of free energy identities such as the Jarzynski equality and the Crooks theorem; consistency theorems (KL distance from the true distribution below ε ⟹ ΔG error below f(ε)). Going by the curves in §17, it is reasonable to expect type A problems like these to start falling in batches from mid-2027 [speculation]. **(b) Other abilities: the main battlefield.** The shift in thinking is to learn the distribution directly instead of simulating trajectories. Microsoft Research's **BioEmu** [recent] was trained on more than 200 milliseconds of MD trajectories + static structures (including the 100 million+ proteins in the AlphaFold database) + about 500,000 experimental stability measurements. On a single GPU it **generates thousands of statistically independent configurations per hour**, reaches relative free energies at the **1 kcal/mol** level, and can capture **cryptic pocket formation, local unfolding and domain rearrangement**. Official materials say it compresses several GPU-years into less than 1 GPU-hour, a speedup of about 100,000× (⚠ this figure comes from promotional material / a video transcript, not from the paper itself).

**If solved: first-order impact** Conformational ensembles and free energy differences computed in hours; cryptic pockets go from chance discoveries to systematic enumeration, and the "undruggable" category shrinks sharply [speculation]; allosteric drug design goes from art to engineering; enzyme design gets a real scoring function; IDPs and phase separation go from "can only be described" to "can be predicted."

**If solved: second-order impact** **The bottleneck of Problem 2 opens up.** Design is currently stuck on "no oracle" (no way to score designs in silico); computing free energies accurately = having an oracle = success rates jump from single-digit percentages to a usable level. This is the strongest causal chain within this section. **The failure mode shifts**: once the binding step becomes reliable, clinical failures will show up **more purely** as "the wrong target was chosen," so the Phase II failure rate **will look harder to improve in the short term**: a fixable layer of problems has been peeled away, exposing the layer that can't be fixed.

**Timing** Problems of this kind **can be judged automatically** (they can be compared against MD and experiment, with quantitative error metrics), so they belong to the class AI will crush. **Optimistic** by end of 2027: ΔΔG for common globular proteins reaches "can replace experiment" accuracy (<0.5 kcal/mol) and enters the standard workflow at pharma companies.

**Counterarguments and uncertainty** The "1 kcal/mol" may have been measured on an easy subset (⚠ BioEmu's performance on hard classes such as membrane proteins, large allosteric machines and glycosylated proteins is unverified). Generative models learn the training distribution, not physics; out of distribution they may be confidently wrong, and "knowing what they don't know" is not yet solved.

**Signals to watch** Pharma companies write conformational ensemble prediction into standard decision workflows rather than treating it as an exploratory tool; prospective blind tests targeting dynamics appear (CASP adding a conformational-ensemble track would be a strong signal); accuracy moves from 1 to 0.3 kcal/mol; a drug driven by a cryptic pocket enters the clinic and **succeeds**. The reverse signal: in 2027 people are still gaming the same metrics on the same set of public proteins.

**Sources**: Abramson et al., *Nature* 630:493–500 (2024), DOI 10.1038/s41586-024-07487-w; Lewis, Hempel, Jiménez-Luna et al., "Scalable emulation of protein equilibrium ensembles with generative deep learning," *Science* (2025), DOI 10.1126/science.adv9817; Bryngelson & Wolynes, *PNAS* 84:7524–7528 (1987).

## 20.3 Problem 2: De novo protein design: binders, enzymes, and the real bottleneck of "no scoring function"

**In one sentence** We can already design entirely new proteins from scratch that bind a specified molecule or even catalyze a specified reaction, but success rates are only a few percent to a few tens of percent. The bottleneck is that **we can't judge in silico whether a design is good**, so we can only synthesize and test in bulk. This is an experimental throughput problem and has nothing to do with math.

**Background** **De novo design** means stating the function you want first, then computing a sequence that has never appeared in nature (in contrast to traditional directed evolution: random mutation + screening). The two kinds of targets differ in difficulty by an order of magnitude. A **binder** must bind a specified target specifically; the job is to match shape and charge at the interface, and this is now fairly mature. **Enzymes** are much harder, because catalysis is not about "binding tightly" but about **stabilizing the transition state more than the substrate and the product**: a catalyst's job is to make "that distorted, short-lived configuration halfway through" sit more comfortably than the substrate does. If the pocket is too friendly to the substrate, the substrate doesn't want to change (the "substrate trap"). So a few catalytic residues have to be placed at specific positions and orientations, with an error tolerance of about **0.5–1 ångström and a dozen or so degrees**, while the whole backbone keeps jiggling at room temperature with a larger amplitude than that. The technical route is diffusion models: **RFdiffusion** from the Baker lab (the Institute for Protein Design, IPD) takes the diffusion models used for image generation and applies them to protein backbones, denoising step by step from noise, with **conditioning** ("these three residues must sit at these coordinates"); ProteinMPNN then fills in the sequence.

**Two results from 2025: the numbers alone show where the bottleneck is.** (a) **Designed serine hydrolases** [recent: Lauko, Pellock et al., *Science* 2025]: the Cα RMSD between the crystal structures and the design models is **under 1 ångström** (the intended shape really was built); they obtained **five folds not found in nature**; catalytic efficiency reached up to the order of `2.2 × 10^? M⁻¹s⁻¹` (⚠ the exponent was cut off in the search results; check the original before citing); **the key number: in the first round only 1.6% of designs had any catalytic activity.** (b) **RFdiffusion2** [recent, IPD announcement 2025-04]: the input is relaxed from "atomic coordinates" to "the chemical transformation I want," and the model decides on its own how to lay out the active site. On a newly built set of 41 enzyme design benchmarks it **solved all of them**, where the previous best tool solved only 16; wet-lab experiments produced active enzymes for 5 reactions, **testing fewer than 100 designs per case**. ⚠ The IPD announcement page lists no limitations; check the bioRxiv preprint for specific numbers.

**How to read these two sets of numbers**: "1.6% active" plus "fewer than 100 designs tested per case" is the whole story: **the design algorithm outputs a pile of candidates, and you can't rank them.** You can only synthesize tens to hundreds of them and see in the wet lab which ones work. So the speed of the whole field = **the throughput of synthesis and testing**.

**Why it matters** Half of the 2024 Nobel Prize in Chemistry went to David Baker (computational protein design) [established].

**What's blocking it: is math the bottleneck?** **Not math at all. The bottlenecks are (1) experimental throughput and (2) the lack of a computational oracle, and (2) is rooted in Problem 1's free energy calculation.** Being unable to score and rank designs is a physics/algorithms problem (= Problem 1), as the 1.6% first-round hit rate shows; throughput is experimental, and "<100 designs per case" directly reflects the throughput ceiling; backbone generation is basically solved (41/41 solved); the catalytic cycle (multiple steps, cofactors, substrates entering and leaving) is a conceptual bottleneck; **math/proof is not a bottleneck: there is no statement here waiting to be proved**.

**What AI can do** **(a) Math/proof ability: almost irrelevant.** The only formalizable angle is "can we give a design a guaranteed statement like 'its minimum activity has some lower bound'?" At present this is entirely impossible, and it may not even be formalizable (a protein's behavior in a cell depends on too much context that can't be modeled). This is a typical example of how scarce type A problems are in biology. **(b) Other abilities: all the leverage is here.** First, **generative design itself** (RFdiffusion / RFdiffusion2 / ProteinMPNN / the ESM family), already in use and already effective. Second, **automated labs**, possibly the most underrated line: OpenAI × Ginkgo (2026-02) had GPT-5 autonomously design cell-free protein synthesis recipes, executed by a robotic cloud lab, over 6 closed-loop rounds, 36,000+ recipes and 580 plates, cutting cost per gram of protein by 40% and raising yield by 27% [recent] (⚠ publication status unclear). It shows that experimental throughput itself can be attacked by AI; if the same loop were used to synthesize and test designed proteins, the limit of "fewer than 100 designs tested per case" would loosen by one to two orders of magnitude. Third, **active-learning loops**: the model proposes → robots synthesize and test → data flow back into training, turning "no scoring function" into "use experiments as the scoring function, cheap enough to afford".

**If solved: first-order impact** Going from "I want a protein that binds X" to "a usable molecule in hand" drops from months to days, and success rates rise from single-digit percentages to tens of percent; most uses of antibodies are taken over by smaller, more stable, cheaper designed proteins [speculation]; industrial reactions not found in nature become designable; diagnostic reagents, biosensors, self-assembling protein nanostructures.

**If solved: second-order impact** **The chemical part of manufacturing starts to "go biological"**: many high-temperature, high-pressure catalytic processes can be done with enzymes at room temperature and pressure, and the chemical industry accounts for something on the order of 10% of global energy consumption. **The toolbox for Problems 3 and 4 fills up**: if you can design proteins, you can make arbitrary intracellular probes, switches and recorders, which directly speeds up the data generation the virtual cell needs.

**Timing** This field **has an automatic judge** (the wet lab), so iteration is fast, but the judge is **expensive**, so speed is set by experimental cost rather than by the AI capability curve. **Optimistic** by end of 2027: binder design is "delivered on demand" (give a target, get a design with nanomolar affinity within a week, success rate >50%), and single-step enzyme hit rates reach 20–30%.

**Counterarguments and uncertainty** "41/41 solved" may mean the benchmark is too easy: the team built the benchmark itself, and calibrating difficulty is an old problem, so wait for independent blind tests. "Active" is a long way from "useful": designed enzymes are typically 10^3–10^6 times less catalytically efficient than natural enzymes, and historically closing that gap has relied mainly on directed evolution (that is, still on experiments).

**Signals to watch** **Independent blind tests** (a CASP-like design challenge, with third parties choosing the targets and third parties doing the wet-lab validation); **hit rates made public** (routinely reporting "tested N, M were active" rather than only the best one); a designed binder entering the clinic; **unit cost at cloud labs** (if "synthesize and test one designed protein" drops from the $1000 level to the $10 level, everything changes); **closed-loop automation used for design, not optimization**: today it mainly optimizes continuous parameters (reaction recipes), and applying it to "discrete design spaces" (which sequence) is the key turning point.

**Sources**: Lauko, Pellock et al., "Computational design of serine hydrolases," *Science* (2025), PDB 9DEE/9DEF/9DEG; IPD, "Introducing RFdiffusion2" (2025-04); OpenAI, "GPT-5 lowers protein synthesis cost".

## 20.4 Problem 3: The virtual cell (short card)

The goal is a model that can answer "if I knock out this gene / add this drug, what happens to this cell?": learn a function `f(cell type, perturbation) → expression change` and extrapolate to perturbations and cell types it hasn't seen (the data come from Perturb-seq, where one experiment perturbs thousands of genes at once and measures the full transcriptome, about 20,000 dimensions, for each cell). **The bottleneck is data and evaluation, not math.** Two 2025 results settled this. The first [recent: Ahlmann-Eltze, Huber, Anders, *Nature Methods* 22:1657–1661 (2025), DOI 10.1038/s41592-025-02772-6] tested 7 models: 5 single-cell foundation models (scGPT, scFoundation, scBERT, Geneformer, UCE) + 2 built specifically for perturbation prediction (GEARS, CPA). **Under double perturbations, every model's error was significantly higher than that of the additive baseline, "add up the log fold changes of the two single perturbations"**; when it came to detecting gene–gene interactions (epistasis), **none beat "predict no change"**. For single perturbations of unseen genes, no model consistently beat a mean baseline or a simple linear model either, and **feeding the foundation models' gene embeddings into a linear model matched or beat the models' own decoders**. The authors conclude that predicting experiments that have not yet been done is still far out of reach; the limitation they state themselves is that they used only 4 datasets, all from cancer cell lines.

The second result is **evaluation metrics gamed until they break**: Arc Institute's first Virtual Cell Challenge newly generated 300,000 H1 human embryonic stem cells with 300 genetic perturbations, and **more than 1,200 teams submitted** [recent]. The score combined three metrics, and on one of them, MAE, **no model beat the baseline**; the organizers then **clipped the penalty at 0**, so "doing worse" was not penalized and MAE in effect **stopped influencing optimization**; one participant named their model **"Leaderboard Hacker"** outright. Another finding: Pearson correlation over all genes, a metric still widely reported today, reaches its maximum on the trivial model that "predicts no change." Arc's own summary says the first year exposed gaps in three areas, model accuracy, metric design and biological generalization, and in 2026 the challenge switched to zero-shot (no training set released). ⚠ Most of these critical analyses come from participants' Substacks and blogs, not from peer-reviewed analysis.

**The key interpretation: this doesn't show that "deep learning is useless"; it shows that current data don't contain enough learnable structure.** The additive baseline is hard to beat because biology really is approximately additive much of the time, while the non-additive part is sparse and context-dependent and, at current data volumes, can't be told apart from noise. This is a clean statistical problem, not a model-capacity problem, so **"AI solving math" does almost nothing for this card**. What is really needed is 10× more data, data with causal design (choosing perturbations by information gain rather than brute-forcing them at random), and metrics that can't be gamed. Math has only a small real role (causal identifiability, optimal perturbation design, and "can we design a metric for which 'predict no change' is not the optimal solution?"; the last can be formalized as a mechanism design problem and **maps directly onto AI evaluation design** [speculation]); compute is not the bottleneck. Resources are pouring in: in October 2026 Biohub's Virtual Biology Initiative expanded to a total of **$1.8 billion**, and its scientific lead Alex Rives said existing data cover about 1 billion cells and that making the models useful will take another order of magnitude (⚠ the $1.8 billion figure appears only in secondhand reports; Biohub's official page gives no amount). **Central timing call: 2030**: reliable for specific cell types + specific pathways, still poor at generalizing across cell types, and the "virtual cell" as a general-purpose tool still doesn't exist; this card depends least on the AI math curve and most on the rate of data generation. The signals most worth tracking are someone independently retesting a new generation of models with the Ahlmann-Eltze protocol and winning, and the results of the zero-shot challenge; among the counterarguments, the one most worth taking seriously is "the transcriptome may simply not be enough" (mRNA is a noisy, delayed proxy variable; if so, the task itself is aimed at the wrong target). **Sources**: Ahlmann-Eltze, Huber, Anders, *Nature Methods* 22:1657–1661 (2025), DOI 10.1038/s41592-025-02772-6; Bunne et al., *Cell* 187(25):7045–7063 (2024), DOI 10.1016/j.cell.2024.11.015; Arc Institute, Virtual Cell Challenge 2025/2026, *Cell* commentary DOI 10.1016/j.cell.2025.06.008.

## 20.5 Problem 4: How the genome encodes a body (short card)

How do the 3 billion base pairs in a fertilized egg grow into a body with hands, feet and a brain? This is biology's biggest **type B problem**. Coding sequence makes up only about 1.5% of the genome; most of the rest is **regulatory sequence**. **Transcription factors (TFs)** bind specific short DNA sequences (usually 6–12 bases) and then recruit or block RNA polymerase; transcription factors are themselves encoded by genes, so you get a **gene regulatory network (GRN)**. Enhancers can sit thousands to millions of bases away and "reach over" through DNA folding to contact the promoter. The classic case is the stripe 2 enhancer of the fruit fly gene *even-skipped* [established]: about 500 bp carry binding sites for the activators Bicoid and Hunchback and the repressors Giant and Krüppel, and the logic is a "squeeze": a broad activation zone is cut once at the front and once at the back, and the narrow band left over is the second stripe. Bicoid concentration decays exponentially along the embryo, and across the roughly 20 μm (3–4 cells) width of stripe 2 it **drops by only 18%**: **an 18% difference in input concentration has to become an "all on / all off" gene output**. That requires extreme nonlinearity, and the parameters of the cooperative effects are almost impossible to measure independently.

**Math is not the bottleneck; concepts are.** The tools (dynamical systems, reaction–diffusion, stochastic processes, Boolean networks) all exist, with room to spare. What's missing is "which one to use, and what to measure." We don't know the right level of abstraction for the developmental program (gene networks? reaction–diffusion of cell-to-cell signals? mechanical forces?); we don't know what to measure (constraining a model would need every TF concentration in every cell at every moment, while what we measure is the average mRNA of a fixed cell population); and we don't know the criterion for success (predicting expression patterns? morphology? phenotypes after mutation? these differ in difficulty by orders of magnitude). Robustness (Waddington's canalization) is itself mysterious, but **this part can be formalized**: it is a structural stability problem for dynamical systems, and a candidate for "turning type B into type A."

The state of the art in 2026 is DeepMind's **AlphaGenome** [recent: Avsec, Latysheva et al., *Nature* 649 (2026), DOI 10.1038/s41586-025-10014-0]: it takes inputs of up to 1 million base pairs and, in a single call, predicts gene expression, splicing, chromatin accessibility, TF binding, 3D contacts and other signals at once. On variant effect prediction it matches or beats the previous best specialized models on **25 of 26 evaluations** (per the *Nature* press release; the June 2025 preprint said 24/26; ⚠ the reason for the difference has not been checked). **The two limitations the authors list themselves are exactly the ones that matter most**: accurately capturing **distant** regulatory elements (for example, 100,000 bases away) is still a limitation; and it has not been validated for predictions on individual genomes. Yet about **90% of disease-associated common variants fall in non-coding regions** [established], often far from the genes they affect; this is the biggest explanatory gap in modern genetics. Another line of work is genomic language models: Arc Institute's Evo series can **generate** genome sequences. In September 2025, using the phage ΦX174 as a template, it generated thousands of candidates; 302 were selected for synthesis, 285 were successfully assembled, and **16 were active** (a 5.6% hit rate; but in the subset with "≥98% sequence identity to the template" the activity rate was as high as 46%; this contrast suggests the model is mostly making small edits near the template, not doing true de novo design) [recent: bioRxiv 10.1101/2025.09.12.675911]. ⚠ Secondhand reports say this work was published in *Science* in August 2026; this could not be confirmed; the 46% vs 5.6% comparison comes from secondhand reports.

**Methodological observation**: models like AlphaGenome succeed by **going around mechanism**. They don't build a GRN; they learn a statistical mapping from "sequence → signal," which works for prediction and fails for understanding. **In biology, AI is routing around type B problems rather than solving them: it gives you an oracle, not a theory.** The central timing call is 2032: predictive models are usable within specific tissues and distance ranges, but the "developmental program" as a **theory** still doesn't exist; the open-problem solve-rate curve **does not apply at all** to this card, because there is no "problem statement" here. **Sources**: AlphaGenome (cited above); Small, Blair, Levine, *EMBO J* 11:4047–4057 (1992); Waddington, *The Strategy of the Genes* (1957); McCarty, "AI-Designed Phages," *Asimov Press* (2025-09-17), DOI 10.62211/21er-45fg.

## 20.6 Problem 5: Clinical failure rates (short card)

The arithmetic of drug development [established: BIO / Informa / QLS, *Clinical Development Success Rates 2011–2020*, 9,704 clinical programs, 12,728 phase transitions]: **the overall probability from Phase I → approval is 7.9%** (the previous edition, 2006–2015, gave 9.6%), and **the narrowest step is Phase II → Phase III at 28.9%**; programs that used patient-preselection biomarkers reached 15.9%, about twice the baseline. Among the causes of failure, **lack of clinical efficacy accounts for 40–50%** ("doesn't work in humans"), toxicity 30%, and poor drug properties 10–15% [established: Sun, Gao, Hu, Zhou, *Acta Pharm Sin B* 12(7):3049–3062 (2022), DOI 10.1016/j.apsb.2022.02.002]. Another clue is human genetic evidence: mechanisms with genetic support are **2.6 times** as likely to succeed as those without [established: Minikel et al., *Nature* 629 (2024), DOI 10.1038/s41586-024-07316-0]. This 2.6× plus the 2× from biomarkers together tell a clear causal story: **the main cause of clinical failure is that we don't know whether this target is really a causal driver of this disease in these patients.**

AI's report card confirms this precisely: AI-discovered molecules have a **Phase I success rate of 80–90%** (40–65% for human-discovered ones), but in **Phase II they fall back to about 40%**, level with the industry average [recent: Jayatunga et al., *Drug Discovery Today* (2024) — ⚠ seen only in secondhand reports; the authors themselves flag the sample size as limited]. **This gap shows that AI is currently solving "is the molecule good?" (a chemistry problem), not "is the target right?" (a biology problem).** So this card's bottleneck is not math at all. It is biological understanding of target causality (= Problems 3 and 4), the incompressible time and sample sizes of human trials, and institutions (Bayesian adaptive designs, platform trials and synthetic controls have existed since the 1990s, but regulators have been slow to adopt them: **the bottleneck is not that the math isn't good enough, it's that the institutions don't accept it**). The only corner of the math with real content is the validity of surrogate endpoints (the Prentice criteria give a sufficient condition, but in practice almost no surrogate endpoint satisfies them; this is a clearly stated, mathematically hard type A problem), but it is not the rate-limiting step.

**The hardest slowdown argument is time constants**: suppose that from today (2026-10) AI made target selection perfect. A new target then takes 1–2 years to reach lead optimization, 1–2 years from preclinical to IND, 1–1.5 years for Phase I, 2–3 years for Phase II and 3–5 years for Phase III, **about 8–13 years in total**; so the claim "AI improved Phase II success rates" **won't have statistically credible evidence until around 2034 at the earliest**, and this argument **does not depend on any assumption about AI capability**. **Central timing call: 2035**: the overall success rate rises from 7.9% to 10–12%, the Phase I advantage is established, and Phase II improves but not dramatically. **In medicine, the exponential curve of AI capability gets eaten by the linear time constants of institutions.** Two things are worth remembering on their own: **clinical trials are the strictest judge for AI-for-science**, because they can't be gamed (double-blind, preregistered, reviewed by regulators), so the clinical success rate is the single most credible indicator of whether AI is really speeding up science; and **the clinical trial system (mandatory registration, CONSORT reporting standards, independent data monitoring) is exactly what the AI eval community is reinventing**, so its failure modes (p-hacking, selective publication, outcome switching) will replay precisely, and its countermeasures can be carried over directly [speculation]. The strongest counterargument is that more than a decade of technical progress has made this number not better but worse (9.6% for 2006–2015 → 7.9% for 2011–2020), the strongest rebuttal to every "this time is different" claim; ⚠ to date no "AI-discovered drug" has completed a full Phase III and been approved. **Sources**: BIO / Informa / QLS, *Clinical Development Success Rates 2011–2020*; Sun, Gao, Hu, Zhou, *Acta Pharm Sin B* 12(7):3049–3062 (2022), DOI 10.1016/j.apsb.2022.02.002; Minikel et al., *Nature* 629 (2024), DOI 10.1038/s41586-024-07316-0; ⚠ Jayatunga et al., *Drug Discovery Today* (2024) (secondhand only).

## 20.7 Problem 6: How the brain computes and learns: and how it relates to AI interpretability

**In one sentence** We now have a complete connectome of the fruit fly (139,255 neurons) and a "wiring diagram + activity recording" for one cubic millimetre of mouse cortex, but **we still cannot derive the algorithm from the wiring diagram** — and that failure has direct implications for how mechanistic interpretability sets its goals.

**Background** **Problem one: credit assignment.** The brain has about 8.6×10^10 neurons and 10^14–10^15 synapses. Learning means adjusting synaptic strengths, and **a synapse deep in the network can only see the activity of the two neurons on either side of it; it cannot see the animal's behavioural outcome.** Backpropagation solves this in artificial networks, but it faces clear biological obstacles [established, review in Lillicrap et al., *Nat Rev Neurosci* 21:335–346 (2020)]: **weight transport** (the backward pathway requires the **transpose** of the forward weight matrix, while biological synapses are one-directional), **temporal separation** (it requires "finish the forward pass → backpropagate → update", which needs a global clock, whereas the brain runs continuously), and **non-locality** (weight updates need a global error signal, but synaptic plasticity is local). Each candidate answer has its problems: **feedback alignment** (use fixed random backward weights; works in small networks, clearly worse than backpropagation in large networks and on hard tasks); **predictive coding** [Rao & Ballard, *Nat Neurosci* 2:79–87 (1999)] (each layer predicts the activity of the layer below it and sends only the **prediction error** upward, so the error signal is locally available; extremely influential, but both its performance as a learning algorithm and its accuracy as a description of the brain are still disputed); **"infer the activity first, then change the weights"** (prospective configuration; ⚠ this round verified only the authors, journal, volume, issue and pages — Song, Millidge, Salvatori, Lukasiewicz, Bogacz, *Nat Neurosci* 27(2):348–358 (2024); **the title and the specific content were not verified**); **equilibrium propagation** (in energy-based networks the gradient can be read out locally from the difference between two equilibrium states; mathematically elegant, biological evidence thin); **three-factor plasticity rules** (a local Hebbian term × a global neuromodulatory signal; **the strongest biological evidence**, but it only gives you "reward-modulated Hebbian learning", which carries far less information than a gradient and cannot explain how the brain learns complex internal representations). **So the state of play is five or six candidate frameworks coexisting, each with partial evidence, none of them established** — a textbook type B problem: it is not that "a proof is missing", it is that "we do not know which equation to write down".

**Problem two: is a complete wiring diagram enough? There is a forty-year, decisive experimental answer.** The complete connectome of *C. elegans* was published in **1986** [established: White, Southgate, Thomson, Brenner, *Phil. Trans. R. Soc. Lond. B* 314:1–340 (1986)]: 302 neurons, about 7,000 synapses, every one annotated. **Forty years later we still cannot predict the worm's behaviour from that connectome** — because the same wiring diagram with different synaptic strengths, neuromodulatory states and ion channel compositions produces completely different dynamics: **the connectome gives you the topology of the graph; it does not give you the weights on the edges or the transfer functions at the nodes.** In 2024–2025 the scale went up but this problem did not go away: the **whole-brain connectome of the fruit fly** [established: Dorkenwald et al., *Nature* (2024-10-02), a package of nine papers] has **139,255 neurons** and about **5×10^7 chemical synapses**, and was built from **21 million** electron microscope images → automated AI segmentation → **human proofreading**; **MICrONS** [established: *Nature* 640:435–447 (2025), DOI 10.1038/s41586-025-08790-w] covered 1.4×0.87×0.84 mm of mouse visual cortex, about **200,000 cells, 520 million synapses and 4 km of axons** (⚠ the numbers differ between sources; an NIH figure caption says 120,000 cells were reconstructed), and **the key innovation was recording function before doing structure** — calcium imaging recorded the activity of **76,000 cells** watching natural-scene video while the mouse was alive, and only then was the tissue fixed, sectioned and reconstructed by electron microscopy.

**Models constrained by the connectome are the most interesting step.** A **whole-brain LIF model** [recent: Shiu et al., *Nature* 634 (2024), DOI 10.1038/s41586-024-07763-9] built a whole-brain leaky integrate-and-fire model from the wiring plus neurotransmitter identity **with no fitted parameters at all** (synaptic strengths taken directly from synapse counts), and still **predicted accurately** which downstream neurons respond when sugar- or water-sensing taste neurons are activated and which ones are required to initiate feeding, and **verified** this with optogenetics and behavioural experiments. **Connectome constraints + deep-learning fitting** [recent: Lappalainen, Tschopp et al., *Nature* 634:1132–1140 (2024), DOI 10.1038/s41586-024-07939-3] took the measured connectivity of 64 cell types in the fly optic lobe as the structure and **fitted the unknown single-neuron and single-synapse parameters with deep learning** (the training objective was "detect visual motion"); the neural activity the model predicts matches experimental measurements from **26 independent studies**. The **"digital twin"** [recent: Wang et al., *Nature* (2025), DOI 10.1038/s41586-025-08829-y] was trained on recordings from real mice watching movies and can predict **the responses of tens of thousands of neurons to videos and images they have never seen**; **once the shared core is trained it transfers to new individuals, sometimes with only 4 minutes of new recording**. More striking still, this purely functional model can also **run anatomy backwards** — it predicted the cell type, position and connectivity of thousands of neurons, verified against electron microscopy data from the same mouse. **What the three works say together**: the connectome **by itself** does not give you the algorithm, but the connectome **as a constraint** makes models far more credible and far more data-efficient — this is a win for "structural prior + learning", not a win for "reading the algorithm straight off the structure".

**Why it matters** Epistemically this is the question of how matter produces mind; medically, treatment of psychiatric and neurodegenerative disease has long been stuck on "we don't know what goes wrong at the circuit level"; the implications for AI need care — historically, neuroscience's contribution to AI has been **coarse-grained inspiration at the architecture level** (convolutional networks ← simple/complex cells; reinforcement learning ← the TD signal in dopamine), not copying of details.

**What's blocking it: is math the bottleneck?** **Mostly a conceptual bottleneck (we don't know what to write down), with math as the second real bottleneck (we lack the theory) and data third. This is the purest type B problem in the section, but it has a rare property: the type B component could plausibly be converted into type A.** Not knowing which learning rule the brain uses is a conceptual bottleneck (five or six frameworks coexisting for forty years); **the missing theory of "what kinds of learning rule can be implemented locally" is a formalizable mathematical bottleneck**; the wiring diagram not being the algorithm is conceptual plus data; synaptic strengths and neuromodulatory states being unmeasurable is a technical bottleneck (electron microscopy gives morphology but not strength; in vivo recording gives activity but not the full connectivity).

**What AI can do** **(a) Math/proofs: the second most likely place in this section for AI math to really help (the first is Problem 1).** Impossibility and equivalence theorems for learning rules; **identifiability in dynamical systems** (can circuit parameters be uniquely determined from a finite activity recording? this is a system identification problem, there is something provable here, and it tells experimentalists directly "more recording will not help, you need a different kind of measurement"); **equivalence classes of representations** (when do two networks count as "implementing the same algorithm"? **this is the same question** in cross-individual and cross-species comparison in neuroscience and in "did two models learn the same circuit" in AI interpretability, and today's tools — RSA, CCA, CKA — are all heuristic and lack a theoretical basis). **(b) Other capabilities:** **connectome reconstruction is itself an AI result** (the initial segmentation of the fly connectome was done by AI, with humans only proofreading; without deep learning these datasets would not exist — this is the least disputed form of AI's contribution to science); **digital twins as a substitute for in vivo experiments** (4 minutes of new recording to fit a new individual, then any number of stimulus experiments in the computer, turning "one mouse and several weeks per experiment" into "a few seconds"); **connectome-constrained model fitting**; **automated hypothesis generation and literature integration**.

**If solved: first-order impact** Knowing what learning algorithm the brain uses → circuit-level understanding of psychiatric disease → genuinely mechanistic treatment (rather than today's "adjust neurotransmitter levels"); a jump in the performance of neural prostheses and brain-computer interfaces (you know what signal to write in); possibly architectural inspiration for AI (though historically the contribution from this direction has been overrated).

**If solved: second-order impact** **1. It changes how mechanistic interpretability sets its goals.**

| System | Scale | What we have | What we can do |
|---|---|---|---|
| *C. elegans* (1986–) | 302 neurons / 7,000 synapses | complete connectome, complete cell lineage, complete genome | **cannot** predict behaviour from the connectome |
| Fruit fly (2024–) | 139,255 neurons / 5×10^7 synapses | complete connectome + transmitter identity | testable predictions in the **two** best-studied circuits |
| Mouse V1, 1 mm³ (2025–) | 2×10^5 cells / 5.2×10^8 synapses | connectome + functional recordings from the same batch of cells | can predict activity (digital twin), **cannot** state the algorithm |
| Large language models | 10^11–10^12 parameters | **all the weights, all the activations, unlimited interventions, retraining possible** | SAE features, partial circuits, causal intervention at the behavioural level |

**The fourth row is the good news**: artificial networks **win outright** on observability — all weights readable, all activations recordable, interventions free, retrainable ten thousand times with different random seeds, any subset ablatable. So "we don't even understand a 302-neuron worm, therefore we will never understand an LLM" **is an invalid inference**; the epistemic conditions in the two cases are completely different. **But the first three rows are the bad news, and the bad news is more specific**: they show that **"having a complete structural description" does not automatically produce "understanding"**. Understanding requires a **theory** — a set of judgments about which quantities to look at and which abstractions to make. That maps exactly onto the type A / type B distinction: from "given a model, compute its mathematical properties" (type A, where AI will dominate) to "judge what this mathematical quantity has to do with safety" (type B, which humans mostly carry). **Connectomics is a forty-year natural experiment on that same distinction in another field, and the result is: the type B part was not automatically solved by the growth in the amount of data.**

**2. Jonas & Kording's microprocessor paper is the sharpest bridge between these two fields.** In 2017 they took a MOS 6502 microprocessor (whose circuit and function we know **completely**) and applied the standard analysis methods of neuroscience to it — lesion studies (cut a single transistor and see which game breaks), single-unit recording, correlation analysis, dimensionality reduction — then asked whether those methods let you understand the 6502. **The answer is no**: the methods produce a pile of statistically significant but misleading conclusions ("this is the *Donkey Kong* transistor") [established: *PLOS Comput Biol* 13(1):e1005268, DOI 10.1371/journal.pcbi.1005268]. This paper's implications for AI interpretability are larger than for neuroscience, because **AI interpretability is using the same set of methods** (ablation, probes, correlation analysis, dimensionality reduction). It does not say those methods are useless; it says **they need a theoretical framework to interpret their output, otherwise they will systematically produce spurious mechanistic stories.** **3. A few concrete cross-field questions**: can the **validation** methods connectomics has developed (for example using optogenetics to check the predictions of an LIF model) be carried over to interpretability? Connectomics' classification of "cell types" and interpretability's classification of "features" are mathematically the same kind of problem (finding discrete structure in a high-dimensional representation) — can the methods be shared? Most important of all, what would the Lappalainen move of "use structure as the constraint and learning to fill in the parameters" look like applied in reverse to interpretability (posit a circuit structure, fit the parameters, and see whether it predicts the model's out-of-distribution behaviour)? Also, neuroscience has a philosophical literature on "what counts as a good mechanistic explanation" (Craver, Bechtel) that the interpretability community has essentially never cited.

**Timing** **Optimistic** by 2029 several major fly behaviours (feeding, grooming, navigation, courtship) have end-to-end testable circuit models, and the "learning rule" question has a widely accepted answer in **invertebrates**; **central** by 2033 the mouse whole-brain connectome is finished or close to it and local cortical circuits have digital twins, but there is still no consensus on "the brain's learning algorithm" (a 10–15 year schedule + human proofreading as the hard bottleneck + conceptual problems not being solved automatically by data); **pessimistic** after 2040 there is more and more data and no progress in theory, and the field becomes "better and better oracles, no understanding" (the default ending for type B problems, with the forty years of *C. elegans* as precedent). **This is the textbook case of "type B problems land slowly"**: the open-problem solve-rate curve **does not apply here at all**; what is useful is an indirect inference from the METR curve — when AI can autonomously do research tasks that take "a human expert several months" (mid-2027 to 2028), it may start to take on the work of "sweeping the hypothesis space", and "which learning rule the brain uses" happens to be a problem where **the hypothesis space is small but each hypothesis is expensive to test** [speculation].

**Counterarguments and uncertainty** The counter-counterargument to "the connectome is not enough": perhaps *C. elegans* failed because 302 neurons is too few (no statistical regularities to exploit), and a fly with 10^5 neurons is actually easier (population-level statistical descriptions become available). Shiu et al.'s whole-brain model making testable predictions without fitting any parameters supports that optimistic reading — **this is the most important uncertainty on this card.** "Perhaps there is no single learning rule": different brain regions and different developmental stages may use different mechanisms, in which case the question itself is misposed.

**Signals to watch** **the prospective prediction success rate of whole-brain models** (Shiu-style models making predictions about circuits that **have not been specifically studied** and then being confirmed experimentally; this is the cleanest indicator); **an experimental verdict on the "learning rule"** (an experiment appears that can distinguish predictive coding, feedback alignment and three-factor rules in vivo); **how far human proofreading gets automated** (if the molecular barcoding route works, the mouse whole-brain schedule moves up substantially); **the cumulative mm³ curve for the mouse whole brain** (1 mm³ → the 10 mm³ project → 500 mm³; watch the slope); **cross-field citation** (mechanistic interpretability papers starting to cite the methodological literature of connectomics — almost none do now, which is an obvious gap); the contrary signal is that by 2030 the main results are still "bigger connectomes + better digital twins" with no new theoretical framework.

**Sources**: White et al., *Phil. Trans. R. Soc. Lond. B* 314:1–340 (1986); Dorkenwald et al., *Nature* (2024-10-02); MICrONS Consortium, *Nature* 640:435–447 (2025), DOI 10.1038/s41586-025-08790-w.

## 20.8 Problem 7: Aging (short card)

> ⚠ **Verification status of this card**: the citations on this card (DOIs are all given) were **not re-verified** this round; they come from background knowledge rather than from this round's search. Treat every citation as "to be checked", especially the 2025–2026 ones.

Aging is the single largest risk factor in the human disease burden, but the field is stuck on three things: **no causal theory**, **no validated readout**, and **institutionally, aging is not an indication** — and not one of the three is a math problem. The most useful operational definition comes from mortality: the **Gompertz law** [established, 1825] says that adult annual mortality rises roughly exponentially with age, with a doubling time of about **8 years** in humans (if annual mortality is 0.1% at 30, then it is 0.2% at 38, 0.4% at 46, about 3.2% at 70 and about 13% at 86); the mortality rate doubling time is about 0.3 years in mice and about 8 years in humans, a factor of about 27, and that is exactly where the difference in lifespan comes from. The drawback of this definition is that **you have to let a population live until it dies before you can measure it.** In 2013 López-Otín et al. proposed 9 hallmarks of aging, extended to **12** in 2023 [established: *Cell* 186(2):243–278 (2023), DOI 10.1016/j.cell.2022.11.001 — ⚠ not verified], but **that framework is not causal**: it is a checklist, not a mechanism diagram, the 12 hallmarks are interrelated and we **do not know which one is upstream**. The two opposing frameworks — **damage accumulation** (aging is the accumulation of random damage, no program, only entropy, implying it is irreversible and can only be slowed) and **program/information** (aging is the ordered loss of regulatory information, cells "forget" what type they are supposed to be, implying partial reversibility) — imply completely different intervention strategies, and **existing data cannot decide between them**. That is the central fact of this card.

**The bottleneck is the readout, and it is a circular dependency.** The gold standard is to let a group of animals or people live until they die and compare survival curves (3–4 years for mice, decades for humans); what you want is an indicator that can be measured within 6 months and that **causally** predicts lifespan — this is the surrogate endpoint problem, exactly isomorphic to Problem 5. None of the surrogate endpoints in the aging field **has passed causal validation**: in 2013 Horvath used methylation at 353 CpG sites in a linear regression to predict age with an error of about 3.6 years, and it works across tissues [established: *Genome Biology* 14:R115, DOI 10.1186/gb-2013-14-10-r115], but the clock was trained on **age**, so what it measures is "things correlated with age", not necessarily "things that cause aging" — an intervention can turn the clock back without extending lifespan. And to validate whether a clock is a valid surrogate endpoint you need a large number of "intervention → change in the clock → actual lifespan" triples, **and producing those triples requires running the full lifespan experiment**; this circular dependency is where the field is really stuck. The genuinely clean evidence comes from the NIA **Interventions Testing Program (ITP)**: three independent sites, genetically heterogeneous mice, double-blind, pre-registered, **and it publishes failures** — the result is that the great majority of candidate compounds do nothing, and rapamycin is one of the few clear successes [established: Harrison et al., *Nature* 460:392–395 (2009), DOI 10.1038/nature08221 — ⚠ not verified]. **The ITP is the most underrated thing in this field**: it is the aging field's equivalent of the clinical trial system, and the great majority of exciting reports in the field have not been validated through it.

**AI's biggest lever is the readout, and there is a real risk there.** Learning "biological age" from imaging (retinal photographs, MRI), from omics, even from voice and gait is standard supervised learning and AI is good at it; but **this only solves "a better correlated indicator", it does not solve "a causally validated surrogate endpoint" — and if the field mistakes the former for the latter it will waste a decade**, which is what is happening. AI's mathematical ability has almost no role here (the only formalizable corner is the statistical theory of surrogate endpoint validity, shared with Problem 5). The central timing judgment is 2035: several interventions aimed at **specific diseases of old age** succeed (rather than at "aging" itself), while the causal theory is still missing. This card has the weakest relation to the AI capability curves of any in the section — **even if AI can do "a human expert's year" of work in 2028, it cannot make mice live faster.** The two most notable second-order effects: if life-extension interventions are expensive they will become the sharpest form of inequality (the gap in life expectancy between rich and poor countries is already more than 30 years); and this field has the worst hype-to-evidence ratio in the section, which makes it a useful calibration ground — learning to tell "validated by the ITP" from "n=8 mice plus a TED talk" is a general skill for assessing any AI-for-science claim. **Counterarguments**: there may be no "mechanism of aging" to be solved at all — if aging is the superposition of thousands of independent damage processes then there is no master switch, and it is not one problem but ten thousand problems. **Sources** (⚠ none verified this round): López-Otín et al., *Cell* 186(2):243–278 (2023), DOI 10.1016/j.cell.2022.11.001; Yang et al., *Cell* 186(2):305–326 (2023), DOI 10.1016/j.cell.2022.12.027 (disputed within the field); Ocampo et al., *Cell* 167(7):1719–1733 (2016); Horvath, *Genome Biology* 14:R115 (2013); Harrison et al., *Nature* 460:392–395 (2009); NIA ITP.

## 20.9 Problem 8: Biosecurity and dual use: at the governance level

**In one sentence** The capability gains on the previous seven cards are **symmetric**: a model that can design beneficial proteins can design harmful ones, and a model that can generate genomes can generate dangerous ones. The bottleneck on this card is **100% institutional** — the United States still has no mandatory nucleic acid synthesis screening legislation, and the framework update required by executive order is more than a year overdue.

**Background** **Dual use** means that the same technology can serve beneficial purposes and can also be used to cause harm, and the dual-use problem is sharper in the life sciences than in other fields because **biological threats can self-replicate**. The key to understanding the whole problem is **three thresholds**: the **information/knowledge threshold** (knowing what to do), the **materials access threshold** (obtaining the materials) and the **execution capability threshold** (actually being able to do it in a laboratory, which requires a great deal of hands-on skill, i.e. tacit knowledge, traditionally acquired only through years of apprenticeship-style training); **the traditional biosecurity consensus is that threshold 3 is the real bottleneck.** **Threshold 1 has already been lowered substantially, and there is quantitative evidence**: the Virology Capabilities Test (VCT) [recent: SecureBio + Center for AI Safety and others, arXiv:2504.16137] has 322 multimodal questions covering **troubleshooting** knowledge of virology laboratory protocols — the "what do you do when the experiment doesn't work" kind of thing that is precisely tacit knowledge — and was built with the participation of dozens of PhD-level virologists; **the human baseline is expert virologists with web access averaging 22.1% within their own subfield**, while **OpenAI o3 scored 43.8%**; the authors say this exceeds 94% of experts **even within their own area of expertise**, and recommend incorporating LLM troubleshooting ability into existing dual-use life science frameworks. **The importance of this result is that it attacks precisely threshold 3** (troubleshooting knowledge is the bridge between hands-on skill and book knowledge), **while the automated labs of Problem 2 attack another part of threshold 3** (AI designs the experiments autonomously and robots execute them, with human involvement essentially limited to preparing, loading and unloading reagents and consumables). Put these two together and **the traditional consensus's assumption that hands-on skill is the real bottleneck is being eroded** — this is the most important, and least clearly stated, trend in this field.

**Threshold 2 (materials access): the screening mechanism exists, but it has a gap for AI-redesigned sequences.** The way commercial synthesis companies screen DNA orders is to run a **sequence comparison** between the ordered sequence and a database of sequences of concern, flagging or refusing it when the similarity is high (the International Gene Synthesis Consortium, IGSC, has a shared protocol); the technical root of the gap is that **screening is based on sequence similarity while protein function is determined by structure** (the same function can be realized by different sequences with very low sequence similarity; in nature this is called remote homology). A *Science* paper in October 2025 confirmed this gap and patched part of it [recent: Wittmann, Alexanian, ... Horvitz et al., *Science* 390(6768):82–87 (2025), DOI 10.1126/science.adu8578, Microsoft Research "Paraphrase Project"]: using 3 open-source generative protein design models, 76,080 sequences were generated for 72 proteins of concern (mostly toxins) and submitted to the systems of 4 screening software developers; the result was that **several hundred potentially dangerous sequences were not flagged**. The team developed a patch, **3 of the 4 deployed it**, and after the patch the average detection rate across the tools reached **97%** on the subset judged by computational metrics to be more likely to retain wild-type function, with no significant rise in the false positive rate. **A key limitation has to be read together with that 97%**: the study **did no wet-lab validation** — nobody actually tested whether these redesigned sequences retain function. A NIST-led follow-up study therefore noted that the true severity of the gap is unknown, and concluded that **current AI protein design systems cannot yet reliably succeed at both retaining activity and evading detection** [recent: NIST, "Experimental evaluation of AI-driven protein design risks using safe biological proxies"]. **The correct reading**: the gap is real (screening can indeed be circumvented in principle), but the actual present risk is **possibly overstated** by this study, and the major vendors have already patched it; this is **a case where responsible disclosure was done well** — find the gap, notify privately, develop a patch, go public after deployment — and the process itself is worth taking as a template.

**The institutional situation: this is where the bottleneck is.** [recent, source: CRS, "Artificial Intelligence and Biosecurity Issues," IF13269, the July–September 2026 versions] **The 2024 Framework for Nucleic Acid Synthesis Screening applies only to federally funded work**; the private sector relies on industry self-regulation. **EO 14292 (May 2025) required OSTP to replace the 2024 screening framework within 90 days**, and the 2026 policy replaced the 2024 policy on dual-use research but **did not revise or replace the nucleic acid synthesis screening framework**; as of July 2026 CRS said it **could not confirm** whether these two policies had been revised as the executive order required. **There is no federal legislation establishing broad regulatory authority**; oversight rests on a mixture of federal law, federal guidance and self-governance. In **June 2026** a coalition of life science researchers, AI companies and biotechnology developers published an open letter asking Congress to make screening of synthetic nucleic acid orders **mandatory** and to put the responsibility on DNA synthesis companies; Microsoft publicly supported stronger screening rules; Anthropic proposed a framework in June 2026 recommending that government set obligations for frontier developers in four areas: biological weapons, cyber operations, loss of control and automated R&D (⚠ the specific content was not verified). On the frontier lab side, Anthropic activated **ASL-3** with Claude Opus 4 in May 2025 [established], explicitly on the grounds that it **could not rule out** that Opus 4 had reached the ASL-3 CBRN capability threshold (while ASL-4 was ruled out); the measures divide into **deployment standards** (Constitutional Classifiers monitoring inputs and outputs in real time and filtering dangerous CBRN-related information, plus a bug bounty and synthetic training data built from jailbreak data) and **security standards** (hardening internal defences so that model weights are harder to steal) — this was the **first actual activation** of an RSP turning from a theoretical framework into operational policy, and the only instance in this section of model capability directly triggering a deployment restriction. **On the open-weights line**: Evo 2's pretraining data **excluded viruses that infect humans**, but Evo 2 is **fully open-source**, so in principle it could be fine-tuned to design human viruses — **this exposes the fundamental fragility of protections of the "exclude the training data" kind under open weights**: excluding data can stop a model from knowing something, but it cannot stop someone from teaching it.

**Why it matters** It is the first real case of AI capability evaluation driving policy: the activation of ASL-3, the proposal to incorporate the VCT into dual-use frameworks, and the deployment of the screening patch — these three form a complete "evaluate → discover → intervene" chain, and that chain has not yet been built in other areas of AI risk (cyber, persuasion, automated R&D).

**What's blocking it: is math the bottleneck?** **Not math at all. In order of importance: (1) institutions (mandatory screening is not legislated, the framework update is overdue), (2) evaluation science (we don't know how to measure "how much the risk has actually increased"), (3) technology (screening needs to move from sequence comparison to being structure- and function-oriented).** Of these, **"we don't know how to measure the real increase in risk" has the most research room**: the VCT measures knowledge rather than end-to-end capability, and the *Science* screening study did no wet-lab validation.

> "For any sequence S that preserves the function of protein P, the screening system F flags S."

If that statement can be formalized, it is a type A problem — the statement is clear and what is missing is a proof (and the construction of an F that satisfies it); it requires first formalizing "preserves function", which is hard but not impossible (structural similarity plus conservation of the active site can serve as a proxy). And **if** such an F can be constructed and its properties proved, screening goes from "an arms race" to "a line of defence with guarantees" — this is exactly the GSAI (guaranteed safe AI, see §19) line of thinking applied to biosecurity, and the problem here is **far more concrete** than writing a spec for "harmless", so it is more likely to succeed [speculation]. The honest other side: this may simply be infeasible, because "preserving function" cannot be formalized in biology (function is context-dependent — the same protein behaves differently in different hosts and at different doses); if so, screening can only ever be probabilistic, a line of defence that depends on continual patching.

**What AI can do** **(a) Math/proofs: mainly the "screening with guarantees" idea above**, plus **theoretical bounds on the false positive / false negative tradeoff** of screening systems (a statistical detection problem, with provable content, and important in practice — if the false positive rate is too high, synthesis companies will not use the tool). **(b) Other capabilities:** **better screening tools** (moving from sequence comparison to structure prediction plus function prediction; ironic but correct: **the same set of models is both the source of the threat and the defensive tool**, which is the clearest case of "defensive acceleration" / differential technology development); **capability evaluation and red-teaming** (benchmarks like the VCT are themselves an AI safety product, but they measure knowledge rather than end-to-end capability; the next generation needs to measure whether, given model assistance, a person with basic training can complete a complex workflow on a harmless proxy — hard to build but more informative); **anomaly detection and KYC for synthesis orders** (looking not only at the sequence but at order patterns, customer identity and order combinations; a classic anomaly detection problem); **monitoring and provenance** (adding molecular watermarks or barcodes to synthesis products so that products can be traced).

**If solved: first-order impact** Mandatory, technically effective global synthesis screening → threshold 2 is restored; evaluation of the biological capabilities of frontier models becomes standardized and a routine gate before deployment; automated labs (cloud labs) carry screening obligations equal to those of synthesis companies — **essentially nobody is working on that last one at the moment, and on the trend in Problem 2 it will become important quickly.**

**If solved: second-order impact** **It provides a template for AI governance as a whole**: if the chain "capability evaluation → threshold → deployment restriction" works in biology, it will be carried over to cyber, persuasion and automated R&D; conversely, if it fails (screening is shown to be impossible to make effective, or legislation never moves), the whole paradigm of governance based on capability thresholds will be seriously questioned — **this is the most important second-order effect on this card, and it is rarely put this way.** **The open-weights debate will be decided here**: the Evo 2 case (exclude the training data + fully open-source = protection can be bypassed by fine-tuning) is the most concrete instance of open-weights risk, and since the evidence in other fields is murkier, biology is the battleground where the evidence is hardest.

**Timing** **Optimistic** in 2027 the US passes mandatory screening legislation, the major cloud labs adopt equivalent obligations, and structure-oriented screening tools become standard (the cross-industry open letter of 2026-06 shows that industry is already on the same side, which is the most favourable condition for legislation); **central** by 2029 mandatory screening is in force in some jurisdictions (possibly the EU before the US), cloud lab obligations still have gaps, and evaluations are still mostly question-and-answer (EO 14292 being more than a year overdue shows that the executive route does not work and that legislation is needed, and legislation is slow); **pessimistic** by 2030 it is still voluntary screening plus patch-style defence, and legislation is rushed through only after a substantial incident (the default path of technology governance). **The AI capability curve is the clock on the threat side here and institutions are the clock on the defence side, and the two differ in speed by orders of magnitude** — this is the structural asymmetry in this section most worth worrying about: the METR curve doubles every 129 days, legislation moves in years.

**Counterarguments and uncertainty** **The risk may be overstated**: the NIST follow-up study says explicitly that current AI protein design systems **cannot yet** reliably achieve both retained activity and evasion of detection, and historically the main reasons biological weapons programmes failed were engineering and scale-up, not sequence design. **The risk may also be understated**, because (a) the VCT shows the knowledge threshold has been crossed, (b) automated labs are working on the hands-on threshold, and (c) open weights make protections bypassable — and the **combined** effect of these three has never been systematically assessed.

**Signals to watch** **whether the US Congress passes mandatory screening legislation** (the clearest single institutional signal); **whether OSTP updates the 2024 nucleic acid synthesis screening framework** (how overdue it is is itself an indicator of governance health); **whether cloud labs and automated labs are brought under screening obligations** (currently the largest gap); **evaluations moving from question-and-answer to end-to-end** (a public evaluation appears that measures end-to-end assistance capability using safe proxies); **a second frontier lab activating ASL-3-like biology-related deployment restrictions** and publishing its reasoning (this would turn "capability thresholds triggering restrictions" from one company's practice into an industry norm); **data points on the attack-defence race** (another cycle of "evasion method found → patch → detection rate restored"; watch how long the cycle is and how far detection recovers — a shorter cycle or a lower recovery rate is a bad signal).

**Sources**: Wittmann, Alexanian, ... Horvitz et al., *Science* 390(6768):82–87 (2025), DOI 10.1126/science.adu8578; NIST, "Experimental evaluation of AI-driven protein design risks using safe biological proxies"; SecureBio / CAIS et al., "Virology Capabilities Test," arXiv:2504.16137.

## 20.10 Second-order effects in this field

### The bottleneck structure of the eight problems

| # | Problem | Is math the bottleneck? | The real bottleneck | Role of AI math | Role of AI's other capabilities | Central timing judgment |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Protein dynamics and function | **partly** | sampling algorithms + force field accuracy + validation data | medium (bounds on estimators, convergence; type A) | **very large** (learn a generative model that samples directly) | 2029 |
| 2 | De novo design of proteins and enzymes | no | experimental throughput + no computational oracle | almost none | **very large** (generative design + automated labs) | 2029 |
| 3 | Virtual cell | no (one small piece, causal identifiability, is) | data + evaluation metrics | small (identifiability, metric design) | large (large models + automated Perturb-seq) | 2030 |
| 4 | How the genome encodes the body | no | **concepts** (type B) + time-resolved data | small (formalizing canalization) | medium (a sequence→function oracle, but no theory) | 2032 (oracle only) |
| 5 | Clinical failure rate | no | target causality + **incompressible time** + institutions | small (surrogate endpoint theory, trial design, but already good enough) | medium (targets, stratification, synthetic control arms) | 2035 |
| 6 | How the brain computes and learns | **partly** (theory is missing) | **concepts** (type B) + measurement technology | **medium-high** (implementability theorems for learning rules, a B→A opportunity) | large (connectome reconstruction, digital twins) | 2033 (no consensus) |
| 7 | Aging | no | **the circular dependency in the readout** + concepts + institutions | almost none | medium (learning biological age readouts; but don't mistake that for solving causality) | 2035 (specific diseases only) |
| 8 | Biosecurity and dual use | no (one "screening with guarantees" idea is) | **institutions** + evaluation science | small but interesting (formalizable screening guarantees) | large (better screening tools + evaluations) | 2029 (some jurisdictions) |

### Conclusion 1: the direct lever is concentrated at a single point, but its downstream is long

"Math is the bottleneck" only holds up for Problem 1 (protein dynamics), with Problem 6 (missing theory) counting as a half; on the other six cards the bottleneck is data, experiments, concepts or institutions. **So the honest answer to "what would happen to biology if math were solved" is: biology gets a large but very local gift (being able to compute the conformations and free energies of molecules accurately), and its biggest problems do not change because of it.** But the downstream effect of that local gift is bigger than it looks, because it forms a four-link causal chain:

> **Compute free energies accurately** (Problem 1) → **design gets an oracle** (the core bottleneck of Problem 2 is removed) → **experimental throughput rises** (if you can rank, you don't have to test blind — plus automated labs) → **the data bottleneck loosens** (the rate-limiting step in Problems 3, 4 and 7 is exactly data)

**So this one mathematical/algorithmic thing, computing free energies accurately, indirectly drives another four cards through the chain "design → experimental throughput".** This is the most important causal structure inside this section.

### Conclusion 2: what AI gives is an "oracle", not a "theory"

This pattern appears **four times** in this section, and all four times it is **prediction succeeds, understanding fails**:

- **Problem 2**: generative models replaced physical scoring functions and the hit rate went up, but they do not explain
- **Problem 3**: Arc's State model learns the mapping from "perturbation → change in expression" and does not build a mechanistic model
- **Problem 4**: AlphaGenome learns "sequence → regulatory signal" and does not build a gene regulatory network
- **Problem 6**: the MICrONS digital twin can predict neural activity, but like the brain it is a black box

This is not an accident; it is a systematic victory of statistical learning over mechanistic modelling. **The consequence**: biology is manufacturing a set of "biology oracles" — models that can answer what-if but cannot say why — so that (a) the models' out-of-distribution behaviour cannot be anticipated, because we don't know what they learned; (b) the models' errors cannot be diagnosed, only found by experiment; (c) human understanding of biology may stall even while biology's **capability** grows. **This is the same predicament AI interpretability faces, and on the biology side there are more samples and the costs are more real** — the two fields should read each other's literature, and at the moment they essentially do not.

### Conclusion 3: biology's evaluation crisis and AI's evaluation crisis are the same crisis

The Virtual Cell Challenge of Problem 3 is a textbook case: 1,200 teams; a metric wrecked by a design error (MAE truncated at 0); a public submission named "Leaderboard Hacker"; a widely used metric (all-gene Pearson) that takes its maximum on the trivial model that predicts no change. At the same time Problem 5 reminds us that the clinical trial field **has already** built institutions for this class of problem — mandatory pre-registration, the CONSORT reporting standard, independent data monitoring, public registration of results. **So there is a very concrete three-way transfer route: the institutions of clinical trials → the institutions of AI evals; and the failure modes of biology benchmarks → an early warning of the failure modes of AI evals.** The "experiment" on the biology side is larger (1,200 teams actively hunting for holes, bigger than the red team of any AI eval), and the countermeasures on the clinical side are already mature.

### Conclusion 4: AI's exponential capability curve is eaten by biomedicine's linear time constants

| Curve/constant | Rate |
|---|---|
| METR task length | doubles every **129 days** (×7 per year) |
| Solve rate for formalizable open problems | 3% in 2026 → ~45% by the end of 2028 |
| Cost of formal proof | ÷10 per year |
| **Mouse lifespan experiment** | **3–4 years** (incompressible) |
| **Clinical development pipeline** | **8–13 years** (better target → seeing better Phase II numbers) |
| **Mouse whole-brain connectome** | **10–15 years** (mostly human proofreading) |
| **Legislative cycle** | **years** (EO 14292 required 90 days, more than a year overdue) |

**This asymmetry is the most important quantitative conclusion in this section.** Three corollaries: (1) **the right indicator for judging whether AI is accelerating biomedicine is not paper counts or benchmark scores but the outputs that cannot be gamed** — clinical success rates, the Eroom's law curve, the number of positive results from the ITP — and the response time of those indicators is 5–10 years. (2) **The highest-leverage intervention is shortening the time constants, not raising AI capability**: making Phase IIa smaller and faster (as with Insilico's rentosertib, which read out exploratory endpoints with 71 people in 12 weeks), automating connectome proofreading (molecular barcoding), raising experimental parallelism (automated labs) — all of these are **institutional and engineering** innovations, not AI innovations. (3) **In biosecurity this asymmetry is dangerous**: the threat side follows the 129-day curve, the defence side follows the legislative cycle.

### Conclusion 5: this field's biggest contribution to AI safety is not technical, it is institutional precedent

Biosecurity (Problem 8) is currently the only field where "model capability evaluation directly triggers a deployment restriction" has actually happened (Anthropic ASL-3, 2025-05). It is also: the first complete cycle of "responsible disclosure" running end to end in the AI field (find the gap → notify 4 vendors privately → 3 deploy the patch → go public); the battleground where the evidence on "open-weights risk" is hardest (excluding training data ≠ safe, because it can be fine-tuned); a race in which "defensive acceleration" can be tracked quantitatively (the same set of models is both the threat and the defensive tool). **If the paradigm of "governance based on capability thresholds" is going to be validated or refuted, it will be validated or refuted in biology first.**

### Cross-field connection list

- **Physics/statistical mechanics ↔ Problems 1 and 3**: the mathematics of spin glasses underpins the protein folding funnel (Bryngelson–Wolynes 1987), cell types as attractors (Kauffman's Boolean networks), memory states in neural networks (Hopfield) and loss landscapes, all at once. See §13, §19.
- **CS/distributed systems ↔ Problem 6**: "given locality constraints, which learning rules are implementable" is an impossibility problem in distributed computing, of the same kind as FLP and CAP. See §18.
- **AI interpretability ↔ Problem 6**: the forty years of connectomics (302 neurons in *C. elegans*, and still no prediction of behaviour) is the decisive natural experiment for "a complete structural description ≠ understanding"; read it together with Jonas & Kording's 2017 microprocessor experiment. See §21.
- **The science of AI evaluation ↔ Problems 3 and 5**: the metric collapse of the Virtual Cell Challenge is a large-sample natural experiment on Goodhart's law; the clinical trial system is a countermeasure that has already been built.
- **AI governance ↔ Problem 8**: the only real case of a capability threshold triggering a deployment restriction; the battleground where the evidence in the open-weights debate is hardest. See §22.
- **Economics/society ↔ Problems 5 and 7**: the reversal of Eroom's law is the decisive economic indicator for "AI accelerating science"; a longer healthspan would restructure demographic and fiscal assumptions.

### Three-sentence summary

1. **Biology's bottleneck is not proof, it is measurement.** So the impact of "AI solving math" in biology works mainly through one indirect chain (compute free energies accurately → design has an oracle → experimental throughput rises → the data bottleneck loosens), not through directly solving any biological problem.
2. **AI is giving biology "oracles" rather than "theories"**, a pattern that appears four times across the eight cards. It is a real gain in capability and also a real epistemic loss, and it is the same problem AI interpretability faces.
3. **In medicine, AI's exponential capability curve is eaten by the linear time constants of institutions** — so if you want to accelerate biomedicine, the marginal return on changing institutions (smaller and faster Phase IIa, automated proofreading, mandatory screening legislation) is higher than on changing models.
