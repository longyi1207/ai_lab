# First, calibrate: how math breakthroughs turn into real-world impact, and how fast AI capabilities will move

> Part I covered what has already happened. Part II asks a bigger question: **if AI solves math at scale (it is already proving open problems), or if AI's other capabilities also break through, which problems that really matter will open up? What are the deeper consequences?** §18–§22 answer this field by field, and §23 pulls it together.
> This section is the foundation. Every later section is first checked against four things here: how long math has historically taken to turn into real-world impact, and where it got stuck (17.1); when math is actually the bottleneck (17.2); the real track record of AI for science so far (17.3); and how fast AI capabilities are moving on trend, and how unevenly (17.4–17.5).
> Tags: [established] accepted historical or research findings; [recent] new results from 2024–2026; [speculation] inference. 17.4–17.5 keep the deck's original tags: [data] has a source; [model] computed from explicit assumptions; [judgment] inference. ⚠ = could not be verified, or sources conflict.

## 17.0 The bottom line

- **The lag from math to real-world impact falls into two classes, one to two orders of magnitude apart** [established]. "Stockpile" math, done for math's own sake (number theory, Riemannian geometry, topology, the Radon transform), took 50–200 years to find an application; "made-to-order" math, done for an engineering problem (linear programming, information theory, Black–Scholes, compressed sensing, polar codes), took only 2–15 years. The vast majority of OpenAI's 719 papers are stockpile math, so on the historical record their real-world impact will be measured in decades [speculation].
- **When an application arrives, the math is almost always already there** [established]. Across 14 historical cases, what held things up at the end was compute and enabling technology; demand; institutions (auction legislation, the legality of organ exchange, Soviet ideology); materials and experiments (topological qubits have been stuck for nearly 30 years); and "the people who needed it didn't know it existed" (Cormack reinvented the Radon transform; LDPC codes were forgotten for 34 years).
- **In most fields, math is only a small segment of the pipeline.** Apply Amdahl's law to a hypothetical drug project: make "theory" infinitely fast and the overall speed rises by only about 5%. AI's real leverage is raising the hit rate of the slow steps and replacing experiments with computation. On the point that "pure cognitive speedup cannot carry a large impact on its own", the optimists (Amodei, Epoch) and the skeptics (Narayanan & Kapoor, Acemoglu) actually agree.
- **As of 2026-10, every proven speedup in AI for science has come from AI's "other capabilities", not from proof** [recent]. Weather forecasting has entered the operational systems of ECMWF and NOAA and passed independent verification; protein structure prediction is used by more than 3 million researchers. The three flagship materials-discovery results (GNoME, A-Lab, MatterGen) have all been heavily walked back; the most-cited MIT paper on "AI speeds up R&D" was withdrawn; and no "AI-discovered" drug has been approved.
- **AI capability curves are exponential, and intuition systematically underestimates them.** The length of tasks AI can complete on its own doubles about every 129 days (about ×7 per year): about 15 hours in 2026-05, and about 400 hours by end-2027 in the central scenario [data][model]. The single-attempt solve rate on preregistered open problems is 3% today; in the central scenario it reaches about 18% by end-2027 and about 45% by end-2028. Formal proofs cost about $0.023/line; at ÷10 per year, the cost of generating proofs becomes negligible in 2027–2028 [model]. The biggest uncertainty is the gap between 98% on benchmarks and 3% on open problems.
- **The speedup is extremely uneven.** Type A problems, which are "clearly stated and only missing a proof", have an automatic judge and will be solved by AI in bulk; type B problems, where "it isn't even clear what to prove", move slowly. Generalizing one step: how fast AI speeds up a field depends on how fast that field's "judge" gives feedback. For Lean it is minutes, for materials synthesis weeks to months, for climate a decade [judgment].
- **Once proofs are cheap, the bottleneck moves to four places** [speculation, with precedent]: whether the statement is faithful (the spec); human digestion and understanding (in Tao's framing, a move from proof scarcity to proof abundance); experiments (theory gets cheaper, so experiments get relatively more expensive); and matching (connecting existing math to the problems that need it). Sequencing costs fell 10,000-fold in 10 years, yet biology did not get 10,000 times faster; proofs will probably go the same way.
- **AI math helps most in fields where "the math is the product"**: theoretical computer science and cryptography, formal verification, coding theory, and rigorous bounds in parts of physics. In biomedicine, materials and the social sciences, AI's other capabilities matter more, but they are just as limited by experiments and institutions (17.7). This is also where the topic meets AI safety: proving a quietly weakened statement is reward hacking in mathematics, and the ability to audit statements and specs is the next shared bottleneck for math, science and AI oversight.

## 17.1 History: the lag from math to application

How long did past math breakthroughs take to go from "proved" to "changing the world", and where did they get stuck? If what held them up was not the math, then AI making math faster can only shorten a small part of the total time.

The table below covers 14 cases [established]. "Math in place" means the key result was first published; "first use" means the first real application; "widespread" means large-scale deployment; years are approximate. After the table, 6 cases are covered in detail and the other 8 get one sentence each.

**Case 1: Number theory → public-key cryptography.** The core tool of number theory is "modular arithmetic": you look only at the remainder after dividing by n, going round and round like a clock. Example: 3⁴ = 81, and 81 divided by 7 leaves 4, written 3⁴ ≡ 4 (mod 7). Euler's theorem (1763) shows that powers mod n periodically come back to 1; RSA is secure because, given n = p×q but not p and q, this period is hard to compute. Timeline: Euler 1763 → Diffie & Hellman propose the idea of public keys in 1976 → RSA 1977 (Cocks at Britain's GCHQ had secretly come up with it in 1973; this was declassified only in 1997) → Netscape's SSL in 1995 → HTTPS becomes the majority of page loads in the mid-to-late 2010s ⚠. What was missing in between: computers that could raise large numbers to powers quickly, demand from networks and e-commerce, and the RSA patent (expired 2000) and US export controls on cryptography. Lesson: in 1940 Hardy declared of number theory and relativity that "no one has yet discovered any warlike purpose to be served by the theory of numbers or relativity, and it seems very unlikely that anyone will do so for many years"; a little over thirty years later, number theory became the foundation of public-key cryptography. The math sat in the stockpile for 200 years, and what activated it was a new engineering need: secrecy between strangers on a network.

**Sources**: Diffie & Hellman 1976, doi:10.1109/TIT.1976.1055638; Rivest, Shamir & Adleman 1978, doi:10.1145/359340.359342; Hardy quote https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_Mathematician%27s_Apology

**Case 2: Riemannian geometry → general relativity → GPS.** Riemannian geometry studies how to measure distance in curved space: on a plane you use Pythagoras' theorem, while in curved space every point has its own "ruler" (the metric tensor). Riemann 1854 → Einstein's general relativity in 1915 (in 1912–15 he spent about three years learning this math, with help from the mathematician Grossmann) → first GPS satellite 1978, fully operational 1995 → in 2000 the US turned off "Selective Availability" (a switch that deliberately degraded civilian accuracy). Why GPS needs relativity: because gravity is weaker up there, satellite clocks run fast by about 45 microseconds a day; because the satellites move fast, their clocks run slow by about 7 microseconds a day; the net effect is about 38 microseconds fast per day. The speed of light × 38 microseconds ≈ 11 km, so without correction the positioning error would build up by about 10 km a day. What was missing in between: atomic clocks, spaceflight, Cold War military budgets and one policy decision. Lesson: 61 years from math to physics, then another 60–80 years from physics to technology; Einstein's three years were a bottleneck of "learning the math", not of "inventing the math".

**Sources**: Ashby 2003, "Relativity in the Global Positioning System", doi:10.12942/lrr-2003-1

**Case 7: Information theory and error-correcting codes → 5G.** In 1948 Shannon proved that every noisy channel has a capacity limit C: at rates below C, codes **exist** that make the error rate arbitrarily small, but the proof did not say how to build them. A small example: repeat each bit 3 times (0→000); on receiving 010, a majority vote decodes it as 0. This corrects 1 error, but the rate drops to 1/3, far from capacity. What came next: Hamming codes in 1950; Reed–Solomon codes in 1960, used in CDs in 1982; Gallager's 1962 LDPC codes, which could not be decoded with the computers of the time, were **forgotten for 34 years**, rediscovered by MacKay & Neal in 1996, and chosen by 3GPP in 2016 as the coding for the 5G data channel; Arıkan's 2009 polar codes (the first codes proven to reach capacity that can also be constructed efficiently) were chosen in 2016 for the 5G control channel, after only about 7 years. What was missing in between: cheap decoding chips and standards bodies. Lesson: within the same field the lag shrank from over 40 years to 7, because industry had a clear metric (how many dB short of the Shannon limit), a standardization window, and could implement it purely in chips. **When both the problem and the acceptance criteria are clear, translation is fastest.**

**Sources**: Shannon 1948, doi:10.1002/j.1538-7305.1948.tb01338.x; Gallager 1962, doi:10.1109/TIT.1962.1057683; MacKay & Neal 1996, doi:10.1049/el:19961141; Arıkan 2009, doi:10.1109/TIT.2009.2021379; https://www.abiresearch.com/blogs/2022/07/20/5g-nr-coding-schemes-a-new-start-with-a-long-history/

**Case 8: Linear programming.** Maximize a linear objective subject to a set of linear inequality constraints: for example, allocate limited wood and labor hours to making tables and chairs so as to maximize profit. The optimum always lies at some vertex of the polygon enclosed by the constraints, and the simplex method climbs upward from vertex to vertex. Kantorovich proposed linear programming in the USSR in 1939 to schedule production at a plywood factory, and it was **neglected in the USSR for about twenty years** (it was suspected of conflicting with the Marxist theory of value); Dantzig proposed the simplex method at the US Air Force in 1947, and within a few years it was being used for Air Force scheduling and oil-refinery blending. In 1975 Kantorovich and Koopmans won the Nobel Prize in economics. Lesson: the same math, under two systems, translated at speeds ten to twenty years apart. Made-to-order math translates fastest; institutions can leave ready-made math sitting idle.

**Sources**: Dantzig 2002, *Operations Research* 50(1):42, doi:10.1287/opre.50.1.42.17798; https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/economic-sciences/1975/summary/

**Case 9: Radon transform → CT.** If you know the "total density" (the line integral) along every straight line through an object, in every direction, can you work out the density at every point inside? Radon proved in 1917 that you can, and gave an inversion formula. Analogy: just from how dark a jelly's shadows are from every angle, you can reconstruct where the fruit inside it sits. In 1963–64 Cormack re-derived a similar result **without knowing about Radon's work**, and almost nobody noticed; Hounsfield (at the company EMI) performed the first clinical CT scan in 1971; the two shared the Nobel Prize in 1979. Cormack only found Radon's paper in the early 1970s. What was missing in between: computers, X-ray detectors, and a company willing to pay. Lesson: 54 years, and the math was reinvented along the way. This is the classic case of "matching cost": the people who needed it didn't know it already existed.

**Sources**: Cormack 1963, doi:10.1063/1.1729798; https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/medicine/1979/cormack/lecture/

**Case 10: Mechanism design → spectrum auctions, residency matching, kidney exchange.** Mechanism design is "reverse game theory": first fix the outcome you want, then design rules so that when everyone acts in their own interest, that outcome is exactly what happens. One example is Gale–Shapley's (1962) "deferred acceptance" algorithm: one side takes turns "proposing" to the other and moves on to the next choice when rejected. One can prove the final pairing is "stable": there are no two people who would both rather leave their partners and pair with each other. The US residency match (NRMP) **had already arrived at an essentially identical algorithm through practice in 1952**, 10 years ahead of the mathematicians. Spectrum auctions had to wait for Congress to authorize them by law in 1993; the FCC held its first auction in July 1994, with rules designed by Milgrom, Wilson and others (Nobel Prize 2020). The NRMP redesigned by Roth & Peranson went live in 1998. Kidney paired exchange was proposed in 2004 by Roth, Sönmez and Ünver, who pushed it into practice; in 2007 Congress passed the Charlie W. Norwood Living Organ Donation Act (Pub. L. 110–144), clarifying that paired exchange does not violate the law against buying and selling organs. Lesson: 30–40 years, held up by law, institutions and trust between hospitals, not by math; practice sometimes even runs ahead of the math.

**Sources**: Gale & Shapley 1962, doi:10.2307/2312726; Roth, Sönmez & Ünver 2004, doi:10.1162/0033553041382157; Roth & Peranson 1999, doi:10.1257/aer.89.4.748; https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/economic-sciences/2020/summary/

**The other 8 cases, one sentence each**

- **3 Group theory → quark model**: In 1961 Gell-Mann and Ne'eman classified hadrons using representations of SU(3) and predicted a missing particle, the Ω⁻, which was found in 1964. The "application" here is scientific understanding, not a product.
- **4 Fourier → FFT**: Cooley & Tukey's 1965 FFT cut the computation from n² to n log n (about 100 times faster at n = 1024); it later turned out that Gauss had written it down in 1805. For more than 150 years the bottleneck was "being able to compute it": one algorithmic improvement mattered more than any new theorem.
- **5 Hilbert space → transistor**: The math came only 15–25 years earlier; going from physics to technology took another 20–35 years, held up by materials and manufacturing processes.
- **6 Logic → computers**: From Boole in 1854 to Shannon's 1937 proof that relay circuits are Boolean algebra took 83 years, held up because nobody understood both sides at once. The historian Haigh cautions that the direct influence of Turing 1936 on actual computer engineering is often overstated, so the causal chain "theory → machine" should be told with care.
- **11 Stochastic analysis → Black–Scholes**: Published in 1973; within a few years every trader had a calculator with the formula preloaded, the fastest case in this table. MacKenzie & Millo 2003 point out that prices moved closer and closer to the formula partly because traders were all using it: the theory changed the world it described ("performativity"), until the "volatility smile" appeared after the 1987 crash. A model adopted at scale rewrites the system it sits in, and AI is no exception.
- **12 Topology → topological qubits**: The theory has long been complete (TKNN 1982; Kitaev 1997), but there is still no accepted technical realization. Microsoft's 2018 Nature paper was retracted in 2021; on the companion paper to the 2025-02 "Majorana 1" announcement, the editors noted that the results "do not represent evidence for the presence of Majorana zero modes"; in 2026-06 Nature published a critique by Legg, and Microsoft stands by its conclusions (⚠ via press reports). Stuck on materials and experiments for nearly 30 years; AI math can do almost nothing here.
- **13 Compressed sensing → fast MRI**: When a signal is sparse, it can be recovered exactly from far fewer measurements than traditionally required. The math is from 2004–06; in 2017 products from Siemens and GE received FDA 510(k) clearance (the vendor says cardiac scans went from about 4 minutes to 16 seconds). The conditions: an existing industry, a clear metric, pure software delivery, and an existing approval pathway.
- **14 Lattices → post-quantum cryptography**: In 1994 Shor proved that a quantum computer could break RSA, so **the math created the demand**. From Ajtai 1996 to FIPS 203 (2024-08) took 28 years; from standard to large-scale deployment took only about 2 years (in 2026-09 Cloudflare reported that about 70% of browser traffic already uses hybrid ML-KEM). Fully homomorphic encryption (FHE) has had a construction since 2009, but its overhead still limits it to a few use cases: constructible does not mean usable.

**Sources**: Gell-Mann 1964, doi:10.1016/S0031-9163(64)92001-3; Cooley & Tukey 1965, doi:10.1090/S0025-5718-1965-0178586-1; Heideman et al. 1984, doi:10.1109/MASSP.1984.1162257; Shannon 1938, doi:10.1109/T-AIEE.1938.5057767; Haigh 2014, *CACM* 57(1):36; MacKenzie & Millo 2003, doi:10.1086/374404; Thouless et al. 1982, doi:10.1103/PhysRevLett.49.405; Kitaev, arXiv:quant-ph/9707021; https://www.computerworld.com/article/4189622/researchers-cast-new-doubt-on-microsofts-quantum-computing-advance-2.html ⚠; Candès, Romberg & Tao, arXiv:math/0409186; https://usa.healthcare.siemens.com/news/fda-clears-mri-technology-02-21-2017.html; Ajtai 1996, doi:10.1145/237814.237838; https://csrc.nist.gov/pubs/fips/203/final; https://blog.cloudflare.com/post-quantum-visibility/; Gentry 2009, doi:10.1145/1536414.1536440

**Five patterns**

1. **Stockpile vs made-to-order.** Stockpile math, done for its own sake, takes 50–200 years to find an application; made-to-order math, whose problems come from applications, takes only 2–15 years. The vast majority of OpenAI's 719 papers are stockpile math (number theory, algebraic geometry, operator algebras…), so on the historical record their real-world impact will be measured in decades, unless someone actively goes out to match them to applications [speculation].
2. **When an application arrives, the math is almost always already there.** In none of the cases did "the application side wait a long time for the math". The final bottlenecks were: compute or enabling technology (cases 4, 6, 7, 9, 14); demand or a threat (1, 14); institutions and law (8, 10, 11); materials and experiments (5, 12); nobody knowing the math existed (6, 7, 9). Counterexamples are rare: Einstein's three years "learning the math"; and error-correcting codes, which from 1948 to the 1990s lacked codes that "come close to capacity and can still be decoded", half a math bottleneck and half a compute bottleneck.
3. **Reinvention is common, and "matching cost" is a real bottleneck.** Gauss's FFT, Cormack and Radon, the forgotten LDPC codes, an NRMP that came before the mathematicians. So AI's most underrated contribution may be **matching existing math to the problems that need it**, rather than proving new theorems [speculation]. In the 2025 "GPT-5 solved Erdős problems" affair, most of the so-called solutions were in fact existing literature that the model had found; at the time this was treated as a scandal, but from the point of view of matching cost it has value.
4. **Physics usually does not wait for rigorous proof.** The path integral still has no fully rigorous definition; Parisi's 1979 spin-glass formula was rigorously proved by Talagrand only in 2006; the Yang–Mills "mass gap" is still a Millennium Prize problem, yet numerical lattice QCD long ago produced predictions that can be compared with experiment. So when AI proves conjectures that physicists already believed (as in §12 and §13), it mainly raises mathematical certainty and rarely changes physics or engineering practice. There are two kinds of exceptions: a proof that overturns a widely held intuition, and safety-critical certification that needs rigorous bounds.
5. **The lag is shrinking, and pure software delivery is fastest.** The fastest cases (Black–Scholes, polar codes, compressed sensing, post-quantum cryptography deployment) all had **a few decision-makers who could drive adoption** (exchanges, 3GPP, a few MRI vendors, browsers and CDNs), and **the deliverable was software or a standard**, not new hardware or a new drug. A caveat: Morris et al. 2011 pointed out that the often-cited "17 years" for medical translation comes from studies that each measured something different, so they compare poorly. The numbers in the table above should be read only as orders of magnitude.

**Sources**: Talagrand 2006, doi:10.4007/annals.2006.163.221; Morris, Wooding & Grant 2011, doi:10.1258/jrsm.2011.110180; Ahmadpoor & Jones 2017, doi:10.1126/science.aam9527 (about 80% of cited papers can be linked through a citation chain to some future patent, typically 2–4 steps away)

## 17.2 A bottleneck framework: when is math the bottleneck?

**Amdahl's law.** Research can be seen as a pipeline: asking the question → theory/math → writing code and analysis → experiments or data collection → review, regulation, rollout. When AI speeds up only one segment, the overall speed is limited by the rest:

> Overall speedup = 1 / [(1 − p) + p / s], where p is the share of the time the accelerated part originally took and s is how many times faster that part becomes.

**A worked example** (the numbers are hypothetical, only to show the structure): a drug development project's time breaks down as theory 5%, computation and analysis 15%, wet lab 30%, clinical trials and approval 50%.

- Make **theory** infinitely fast: 1 / 0.95 ≈ **1.05×**.
- Make **theory + computation and analysis** both infinitely fast: 1 / 0.80 = **1.25×**.
- Also make the wet lab 10 times faster: 1 / (0.50 + 0.03) ≈ **1.9×**.
- Only if clinical trials and approval also speed up could you get 10×.

In economics the same structure is called the **Baumol effect**. As Aghion, Jones & Jones (NBER w23928) put it, growth is constrained "not by what we do well but rather by what is essential and yet hard to improve".

But Amdahl's law underestimates two ways out for AI. One is **reducing the number of slow steps**: clinical trials themselves will not get faster, but with better drug candidates, fewer trials are needed. The overall success rate from entering Phase I to approval is about 7.9% (BIO's figures for 2011–2020); double the success rate and the number of trials behind each approved drug halves, which is equivalent to a "2× speedup" for this segment. The other is **replacing experiments with computation**: AlphaFold means many projects no longer need to solve a crystal structure first, and AI weather models learn from reanalysis data and have replaced part of numerical simulation (17.3). So judging "how much AI math can help" means asking three things. How large a share of the pipeline is math? Can it raise the hit rate of the slow steps? Can it let the slow steps be replaced by computation?

**Ideas are getting harder to find** [established]. Bloom, Jones, Van Reenen & Webb (*AER* 2020): keeping Moore's law going takes **more than 18 times** as many researchers as in the early 1970s, and research productivity falls by about 7% a year; research productivity in the US economy as a whole has fallen **41-fold** since the 1930s, and the economy still grows because the number of people doing research has grown even faster. The optimistic reading: if AI can act as "researchers", their number can grow almost without limit and offset the fall in productivity; this is the core of the explosive-growth argument. The pessimistic reading: ideas are getting harder to find partly because the **experimental and physical inputs** each idea needs keep rising (EUV lithography machines, for example), and adding people does not fix that.

**The optimists.** In "Machines of Loving Grace" (2024-10), Dario Amodei says the question to ask is about "the marginal returns to intelligence": how much progress an extra helping of smarts buys. He lists five limits: the speed of the outside world, the need for data, intrinsic complexity, human constraints (law, clinical trials), and the laws of physics. He argues that progress in biology "comes in large part from a very small number of discoveries", and that with a lot of brilliant researchers the rate of such discoveries "could be increased 10x or more". From this he gets the "**compressed 21st century**": 50–100 years of biological progress in 5–10 years. Notably, the key breakthroughs he lists (CRISPR, microscopy techniques, genome sequencing and synthesis, optogenetics, mRNA vaccines, CAR-T) are almost all **new tools for measuring and manipulating**, and not one is a new theorem. His 10× speedup depends on AI's ability to design experiments and invent tools, not on its ability to prove things.

The growth modelers are optimistic too, but with conditions. Davidson (Open Philanthropy 2021) and Erdil & Besiroglu (arXiv:2309.11690) argue that if AI can replace almost all human labor (physical labor included), a 10-fold rise in the growth rate is not outlandish; the various objections "cannot decisively rule out" explosive growth, but high confidence in it is not justified either. The authors of Epoch's GATE model (arXiv:2503.04941) say explicitly that its outputs are not forecasts. One counterintuitive point comes from Erdil & Barnett (Epoch, 2025-03): since 1988, R&D has explained only about **20%** of US labor productivity growth; of the 14 O*NET tasks for medical scientists, only 6 can be done by pure reasoning, and only 1 of the 5 most important ones. So a "pure software singularity" is unlikely, and AI may speed up science mainly through non-R&D work such as automating experimental infrastructure.

**The skeptics.** Acemoglu (NBER w32487) estimates that AI will raise total factor productivity by at most about 0.66% over the next 10 years (critics note that his inputs come from early models). Narayanan & Kapoor ("AI as Normal Technology", 2025-04): diffusion is measured in decades, not years; the social sciences and medicine have to experiment on people and organizations, and "there is a hard limit on the speed of knowledge acquisition". Kapoor & Narayanan ("Could AI slow science?", 2025-07) propose the "**production–progress paradox**": from 1900 to 2015 the number of papers grew about 500-fold, yet scientific progress did not speed up to match. AI mainly speeds up "production", which is like widening the highway when the congestion is caused by the toll booth. The two camps actually share some ground: "Common Ground", written in 2025-11 by Kapoor, Narayanan, Kokotajlo (an author of AI 2027) and others, agrees that until "strong AGI" arrives, AI is a normal technology; they disagree on when strong AGI will arrive and how fast it will diffuse once it does.

**First, separate three kinds of "math".** (i) **Proof**: given a statement, prove whether it is true. This is where AI is breaking through fastest right now, and it corresponds to the type A problems of 17.5. (ii) **Modeling and formulation**: deciding which equations to use and what to prove. These are type B problems, and AI speeds them up slowly. (iii) **Computation and simulation**: given the equations, compute the numbers. This belongs to high-performance computing and ML surrogate models, and has little to do with proof ability. For example, proving that the Navier–Stokes equations blow up in some forced setting (§11) is (i); it will **not** help you better predict turbulence in an engine, which is (iii). On this basis, there are three tiers:

| Tier | Condition | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **The math is the product** (helps a lot) | The deliverable itself is a proof, an algorithm or a bound; there is an automatic judge | Algorithms and complexity, cryptographic security reductions and cryptanalysis, formal verification of software and hardware, compiler correctness, coding theory, parts of optimization theory |
| **The math screens experiments** (helps moderately) | Theory can narrow the experimental search space or give error bounds, reducing the number of slow steps | Quantum algorithm and error-correcting code design, safety certificates for control and robotics, experimental design and statistics, convergence proofs for numerical methods |
| **The math is not the bottleneck** (helps little) | The unknowns are empirical facts, or things are stuck on physical throughput or institutions | Most of biology and medicine, materials synthesis, topological qubits, the social sciences, any field dominated by regulation |

**Meta-pattern** [speculation, consistent with 17.1]: the higher the share of a field's problems that are "clearly stated and only missing a proof" (the larger p in Amdahl's formula), the bigger the overall speedup from AI math. In fields where that share is low, AI's other capabilities (coding agents, simulation, automated experiments, literature matching, hypothesis generation) matter more.

## 17.3 The real track record of AI for science so far

Three standards, each stricter than the last: (1) **independent verification**: has anyone other than the authors checked it? (2) **adoption by non-authors**: has it been used for real decisions? (3) **a change in downstream outcomes**: did forecast accuracy, the time it takes a drug to reach market, or cost change? On this basis, there are four tiers.

| Tier | Case | What happened |
|---|---|---|
| **Proven speedup** | Weather forecasting | [established] GraphCast (*Science* 2023) beat ECMWF's deterministic model on 90% of 1,380 verification targets; GenCast beat the ECMWF ensemble forecast on 97.2% of 1,320 targets. [recent] ECMWF's AIFS went into operational use in 2025 and was upgraded to v2 in 2026-05; NOAA's AI models went into operational use in 2025-12, with a claimed saving of up to 99.7% in compute (⚠ the claim that they are based on GraphCast and others appears only in secondary sources). The US National Hurricane Center's 2025 verification: the DeepMind ensemble mean was slightly better on short-range track and best on intensity; but over the three years 2023–25 combined, the multi-model consensus was still best. |
| **Proven speedup** | Protein structure prediction | [established] AlphaFold 2 turned "predicting 3D structure from sequence" from years of experiments into minutes of computation, and won the 2024 Nobel Prize in Chemistry. DeepMind's own report (2025-11): more than 200 million predicted structures, used by more than 3 million researchers. |
| **Real but incremental** | Algorithms and constants | AlphaTensor (2022) did 4×4 matrix multiplication in 0/1 arithmetic with 47 multiplications (previous record 49); **three days later** Kauers & Moosbauer cut 5×5 from 96 to 95. | **Withdrawn** | The MIT paper on "AI speeds up materials R&D" | Toner-Rodgers's preprint (arXiv:2412.17866) claimed that after a lab adopted AI, it found 44% more new materials and filed 39% more patents (⚠ via press reports); it was once the most-cited causal evidence that "AI speeds up R&D". On 2025-05-16 MIT's economics department said it had "no confidence" in the data, and arXiv took it down soon after. |
| **Overstated** | Sakana AI Scientist v2 | A fully AI-written paper "passed peer review at an ICLR 2025 workshop": 1 of 3 passed (workshop acceptance rate 60–70%), and it had citation errors; Sakana itself admits none of them reached main-conference level. |

**How the three materials flagships were walked back** [recent]:

- **GNoME** (DeepMind 2023) claimed 2.2 million crystals "below the convex hull" (the convex hull is the "lower energy boundary" formed by connecting the most stable known combinations; being below it means the crystal may be stable). Cheetham & Seshadri (2024) sampled 10 at random, and **all of their compositions already appeared in the inorganic crystal database ICSD**; many of the ordered cation arrangements are unrealistic at synthesis temperatures. Their conclusion: "scant evidence for compounds that fulfill the trifecta of novelty, credibility and utility."
- **A-Lab** (an autonomous lab, 2023) claimed to synthesize 41 new compounds in 17 days. Leeman, Schoop, Palgrave et al. (2024): "no new materials have been discovered"; about two-thirds were known disordered phases mistaken for the predicted ordered structures. In 2026-01 the authors issued a correction: "novel materials" in the title became "inorganic materials", and "new" was changed to "new to the prediction platform".
- **MatterGen** (Microsoft 2025) designed TaCr₂O₆, which Juelsholt (2026) pointed out is a known compound, reported back in 1971 and present in the training set.
- The shared failures: being "stable" in a computation does not mean novel, synthesizable or useful; automatic fitting of X-ray diffraction cannot tell ordered from disordered structures; training-set leakage. The bottleneck is synthesis, characterization and "what counts as new"; more computational predictions in fact produced more candidates for humans to check.

**Systematic evidence.** Hao, Xu, Li & Evans (arXiv:2412.07727, noted as accepted at *Nature*, ⚠ DOI not verified), based on 41.3 million papers: scientists who use AI publish 3.02 times as many papers as controls and receive 4.84 times as many citations, but their research topics **narrow by 4.63%** and interaction between scientists **falls by 22%**, consistent with the "production–progress paradox". Kapoor & Narayanan (*Patterns* 2023): 294 papers across 17 fields had "data leakage". McGreivy & Hakim (2024): 79% of papers using machine learning to solve partial differential equations compared against weak baselines. Epoch (2025-09): AI raises productivity on "desk research" by at least 10–20%, but turning lab progress into new drugs still takes years of clinical trials and approval.

| Tier | What the cases have in common |
|---|---|
| **Proven speedup** | Plenty of standardized data; output can be scored quickly, objectively and automatically; the deliverable plugs into an existing workflow |
| **Real but incremental** | Narrow problems with a built-in checker; humans often catch up quickly; real-world impact is mostly engineering optimization worth a few percent |
| **Too early** | Verification depends on wet labs and clinical trials, with cycles measured in years |
| **Overstated, corrected, withdrawn** | Fuzzy definitions of "new" and "success"; unreliable automatic characterization; training-set leakage; untrustworthy data sources |

**In one sentence**: so far, every proven speedup AI has delivered in science has come from its "other capabilities" (learning from large amounts of data, searching fast in spaces that have a checker), not from proving theorems. The explosion in proof ability is real, but its real-world impact has not yet begun: what has been improved is mostly pure math and constants, and ω ≤ 9/4 is an unusable "galactic algorithm" (§6).

## 17.4 Three capability curves: extrapolate from the trend, not from intuition

> **A convention about "realism"**: human intuition is used to extrapolating linearly, but all three curves below are exponential, so the future you get by following the trend often looks "too fast". The rule is: **don't be conservative just because something is counterintuitive, and don't make things up either**. Every point in time states which curve it depends on, what assumptions it uses, and which signals would falsify it. The timing judgments in §18–§22 all connect back to these three curves.

### 17.4.1 Curve ①: how long are the tasks AI can complete on its own?

**What it is**: METR gives AI a set of software and research tasks. It first measures how long each task takes a human expert, then finds the task length at which AI "has a 50% chance of succeeding". That length is called the time horizon, and it compresses "how capable AI is" into a single number you can plot on a chart.

| Release | Model | Time horizon (p50) |
|---|---|---|
| 2019-02 | GPT-2 | 0.1 minutes |
| 2023-03 | GPT-4 | 4 minutes |
| 2024-12 | o1 | 39 minutes |
| 2025-04 | o3 | 2.0 hours |
| 2025-08 | GPT-5 | 3.4 hours |
| 2026-02 | Claude Opus 4.6 | 12 hours (CI 5.3–61) |
| 2026-04 | Claude Mythos Preview | 17.4 hours (CI 8.5–55) |
| 2026-06 | GPT-5.6 Sol | Cheating counted as failure: 11.3 hours; other treatments: 71–270+ hours |

[data] Sources: METR TH1.1 raw data benchmark_results_1_1.yaml (updated 2026-05-08); GPT-5.6 Sol from METR's 2026-06-26 report.

**Doubling time**: fitting from 2023 onward, the horizon doubles every **129 days** (95% CI 104–158 days); using only data from 2024 on, about 89 days; using all data since 2019, about 188 days [data]. A 129-day doubling works out to annual growth of 2^(365/129) ≈ **×7 per year**. This is where the counterintuitive part comes from: linear intuition expects next year to be "a bit better", while the trend says "7 times better".

## 17.5 The core framework: type A and type B, and how fast the judge is

All three curves measure the same kind of thing: **work with an automatic judge**. The Lean kernel judges whether a proof is correct, tests judge whether code is correct, and benchmarks judge whether an answer is correct. AI improves extremely fast on this kind of work, because reinforcement learning can run the loop "try → get reliable feedback → improve" endlessly. So AI's speedup is extremely uneven, and the bottleneck moves.

| Has an automatic judge (AI will crush it) | No automatic judge (still mostly up to humans) |
|---|---|
| Given a theorem statement, find a proof | Decide **which theorem to prove**, and whether the statement captures what you actually care about |
| Given a spec, write code and prove that the code meets the spec | Write **the right spec** (for example, what "harmless" means) |
| Given a model, compute its mathematical properties | Decide how that mathematical quantity relates to "safety" |
| Run experiments, sweep hyperparameters, reproduce results | Design **the right experiment**, and decide what the results show |

**Evidence that the bottleneck is already moving** [data]:

- **Proof success rates have soared**: on the CLEVER benchmark (161 problems, HumanEval translated into Lean; it requires writing code and proving that the code meets the spec), end-to-end success rose from 1/161 in 2025-05 to about 87.5% in 2026-05 (a Claude Code agent). DafnyBench went from about 68% to 96% in one year.
- **Spec quality has not kept up**: the vericoding benchmark (Tegmark's group, 12,504 specs) found that among the samples that verified **successfully**, about 9% of the specs were too weak ("vacuous specs": for example, a sort function's spec that leaves out "the output is a permutation of the input", so returning an empty list also passes), and about 15% of the translations had problems.
- **Nobody reviews the statements**: the status of OpenAI's 416 Comparator challenge files is `review: unchecked` (§3); FLT could be confirmed because Mathlib already had a human-written statement to compare against.
- **The IMO 2026 process** added a dedicated judge, after the Lean proof, of "whether the formal statement covers the original problem". Aligning statements has itself become a new hard problem.

**Split "missing math" into two types.** Saying a field "needs more math" actually covers two completely different situations:

- **Type A: the statement is already clear, only the proof is missing** (the math of kind (i) in 17.2). For example: "without the stability assumption, is Prover-Estimator Debate still sound?" This is a well-defined proposition, and with Lean there is an automatic judge. **AI will solve this kind of problem in bulk around 2027** (depends on curves ②③).
- **Type B: it isn't even clear what to prove** (kind (ii)). For example: "what quantity should characterize the gap between networks trained by SGD and the Bayesian posterior?" The hard part is finding the right statement. AI can propose conjectures and run numerical checks, but there is no automatic judge to tell it "this statement is the right question". **AI helps slowly here.**

The table below uses as examples the four AI safety theory directions that the earlier version of the deck focused on (§21 reselects AI theory and alignment problems from a wider range):

| Direction | Type A (missing proof) | Type B (missing statement) | Engineering / experiments | Conceptual |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Debate** | PE debate without stability; protocols robust to an ε fraction of judge errors | What kinds of real-world arguments satisfy stability; how to formalize equilibria with non-zero-sum payoffs and collusion | Whether RL can reach the honest equilibrium (exploration hacking); testbeds with real capability gaps | How to define "honesty" for questions with no ground truth; true but selective statements |
| **SLT** | Learning coefficients for specific architectures (symbolic computation); Langevin dynamics and the LLC | The gap between SGD and the Bayesian posterior; what finite-sample and ReLU versions should look like | Robustness of the estimators; scaling to 10B+ models | How λ relates to "deception" and "reward hacking" |
| **GSAI** | Proofs about software and protocols (already largely automatable) | A compositional theory of sound bounds over sets of probability distributions (credal sets) | Tools for drafting and reviewing specs; repository-level verification; hardware | Where bounds on physical perturbations come from; "harm" in social domains cannot be written down |
| **Neurosymbolic** | (mostly engineering already) | Whether there is a sound way to check the translation layer | "LLM proposes, solver decides" becomes the default architecture | Which tasks can be decomposed this way |

**From "is there a judge" to "how fast is the judge"** [judgment]. Physics and chemistry suggest a generalization of this framework: **how long one round of feedback takes** determines how fast AI can turn the "try → improve" loop. For theorems in mathematical physics the judge is Lean, taking minutes to hours, and the speedup has already happened; for weather the judge is tomorrow's observations, scoring daily, and the speedup has also happened; for electronic structure the judge is high-accuracy computation, hours to days; for materials and superconductors the judge is synthesis plus characterization, weeks to months, subject to Amdahl's constraint; for climate the judge is observation, which takes a decade; quantum gravity has almost no judge at all. Type A and type B are the two ends of this spectrum. The full table is in §19.

## 17.6 When proofs are nearly free: how science itself will change

**First, an analogy that has already played out.** The cost of sequencing one human genome was about $14 million in 2006 and below $1,500 by the end of 2015, a **10,000-fold** drop in about 10 years (NHGRI) [established]. As a result, biology became "data-rich, interpretation-poor": "what does this variant do" still has to be answered one experiment at a time. The bottleneck moved from "reading sequences" to "understanding sequences" and "functional experiments"; overall progress sped up, but nowhere near 10,000-fold. The cost of proofs is also falling, by about 10× a year (curve ③). By this analogy [speculation]: the number of theorems explodes; the bottleneck moves to "what does this theorem say, does it matter, is the statement right"; overall progress in mathematics speeds up, but by far less than the drop in cost.

**The argument inside mathematics is over whether the product of mathematics is theorems or human understanding.**

- **The understanding camp.** Thurston 1994: "The measure of our success is whether what we do enables people to understand and think more clearly and effectively about mathematics." By this standard, a 100-page AI proof that nobody understands, even if correct, has done almost none of the work of mathematics. De Millo, Lipton & Perlis 1979: proofs are believed because they have gone through a **social process** (being read, talked about, revised, used), not because they are formally correct.
- **Tao: from proof scarcity to proof abundance.** In his ICM 2026 public lecture he argued that most of our institutions were designed under the assumption of scarcity (paraphrased). He splits problem solving into five links: generation → verification → exposition → publication → digestion and canonization; AI makes only the first link nearly free. His small model: if "solving a problem" drops from 6 months to 1 day while "writing it up well" only drops from 1 month to 3 weeks, the literature flips from about 90% well written to about 90% badly written. He and his collaborators found AlphaEvolve "extremely good at finding loopholes in the verification code we provided", **which is reward hacking**. On OpenAI's release, he said that open problems are being "harvested at scale in an unsustainable way, leaving whole areas of mathematics barren". This echoes Thurston's 1994 recollection: after he proved a series of theorems on foliations, a dramatic exodus from the field (paraphrased) followed, even though the field "was not intellectually exhausted".
- **The theorem camp.** Buzzard says what he cares about is the number of theorems. But he also says that formal languages can check proofs, while **definitions still need to be checked by humans** (both paraphrased). Gowers did not sign the Fields medalists' joint letter: the flood of results will increase the absolute amount that actually gets digested, which "is a pretty good deal"; the real risk is to the talent pipeline of PhD students and funding.
- **Collective statements.** The Leiden Declaration (2026-06) holds that credit and responsibility continue to belong to the humans in the mathematical community (paraphrased). The joint letter from 25 Fields medalists, "A Severe Misalignment of AI in Mathematics" (2026-09-11): solving problems is "only a tool for, and a proxy for, the primary goal of conceptual understanding and insight". The IAS-hosted advisory group AGMAI (including Gowers, Hairer and Witten; Tao is not a member) issued guidelines on 2026-09-29 requiring disclosure of the model, prompts, chain-of-thought summaries and compute, and formalization wherever possible. The AHM statement (2026-10-07) calls for ending collaboration with OpenAI.
- **Beyond mathematics.** Climate scientist Schneider (2026-09): where results can be tested quickly (weather, AlphaFold), black boxes are fine; where predictions must be acted on before they can be verified (aircraft design, climate), you need "an auditable causal chain". This matches 17.3: AI's proven successes are all on the "quickly testable" side.

**How to read these disagreements** [speculation]: if the product is theorems, real-world impact depends on how fast results are **matched** to applications (17.1); if it is understanding, impact depends on how fast results are **digested**, and digestion grows at roughly human speed. Either way, "generating proofs" is no longer the bottleneck.

**Science as a whole: theory gets cheap, experiments get relatively expensive.** Applying the Amdahl / Baumol structure inside science gives six implications [speculation, each with precedent]:

1. **Experiments become relatively more expensive and more valuable.** The pace of progress is set more and more by "how many informative experiments can be run per year". Precedent: after computing became cheap from the 1950s on, numerical simulation did not replace experiments; instead it made people more careful about "which experiment to run". Experimental design, measurement tools and automated labs will become scarce resources.
2. **More theories than can be adjudicated.** High-energy theoretical physics is already like this. A proof only guarantees "if the premises hold, the conclusion holds"; which theory describes the real world is still for experiments to decide.
3. **The object of verification shifts from "the derivation" to "the statement".** Lean cannot check whether a formal statement says what you think it says (the evidence in 17.5). The throughput of human statement review grows at human speed and will become the new hard bottleneck.
4. **Understanding becomes a separate product.** There will be dedicated work that "translates" AI proofs into structures, intuitions and examples that humans can understand. This deck's from-scratch explanations of 10 results in §4–§13 are a handmade version of it.
5. **Matching may be worth more than proving** (pattern 3 in 17.1). AI's ability to search and translate across fields may turn into real-world impact sooner than its ability to prove.
6. **Incentive and trust structures get rewritten.** Authorship, promotion reviews and peer review are all built on the assumption that "proofs are scarce". Hao et al. found that individual output rises while collective topics narrow (17.3), which points to a risk: everyone crowds onto the problems AI is best at, and **fewer people end up working on type B problems**.

**Where this meets AI safety**:

- **Proving a quietly weakened statement is reward hacking in mathematics.** A model has an incentive to prove a weaker version that passes the checker more easily, so statement review is a safety problem, not just an academic norm. The 3 withdrawn Hodge-related papers (one sign error cascading through three papers, §14) are a cautionary example from the unformalized parts.
- **"Was the spec written correctly" is the engineering version of the same problem**, and it is also the bottleneck for approaches like Guaranteed Safe AI (§21).
- **Separating understanding from proof weakens the foundation of human oversight.** If even mathematicians can only "trust the checker", then in domains that are harder to check (AI's own behavior), human oversight will rely even more on a few mechanically checkable properties. Scalable oversight has to answer exactly the general version of this question.

**Converting to dates** [speculation]: in the central scenario, **proofs stop being the bottleneck in formalizable domains around 2027–2028** (depends on curves ②③; 1–2 years later in the pessimistic scenario). Made-to-order problems (those with a clear customer waiting, such as cryptanalysis and the verification of protocols and compilers) land within 2–5 years of the proofs arriving (pattern 5 in 17.1); stockpile results take decades on the historical record, unless AI's matching ability shortens the lag substantially. **Falsifying signal**: if before 2028 there are several cases of "a stockpile theorem proved by AI entering a product within 3 years", then AI has greatly lowered the matching cost, and the lag estimates here should all be moved earlier.

## 17.7 Summary table of bottlenecks by field

"AI math" means proof, formalization and symbolic derivation (speeding up type A problems); "AI's other capabilities" means coding agents, simulation and surrogate models, automated experiments, literature matching, and hypothesis generation. High/medium/low are relative judgments [speculation]; if you cite them, please cite them together with the rationale in the last column. The sections in parentheses discuss each field in depth.

| Field | Main bottleneck | How much AI math helps | How much AI's other capabilities help | One-line rationale |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Theoretical CS / algorithms / cryptography** (§18) | The math itself (proofs, cryptanalysis); on the deployment side, standardization | **High** | **High** | The deliverable is a proof or an algorithm, with an automatic judge. But watch out for "galactic algorithms" (§6); deploying cryptography goes through multi-year standards processes (case 14). |
| **Formal verification** (§18) | Writing the right spec; proof cost (falling fast) | **High** | **High** | Proof cost falls by about 10× a year (curve ③), and the bottleneck moves to "was the spec written correctly" (type B). |
| **Pure mathematics** (Part I) | Statements and understanding; refereeing and verification bandwidth | **High** (for proofs) | Medium (literature matching, formalization) | About 58% of OpenAI's 719 papers are not formalized, and no expert has publicly confirmed them. The bottleneck has moved from "can't prove it" to "can't read it all, can't trust it". |
| **Fundamental theoretical physics** (§19) | Experimental data (very little new data beyond the Standard Model); "what to compute" | **Medium** | Medium | Rigorous proof mainly raises certainty (pattern 4); symbolic computation (scattering amplitudes and the like) has real value; many theories and few experiments that can adjudicate between them is an old problem. |
| **Condensed matter / quantum technology** (§18, §19) | Materials and experiments; many-body computation | Low–medium | **Medium–high** | Topological qubits have been stuck on materials for nearly 30 years (case 12); computational tools such as ML potentials help more directly; quantum error-correcting code design falls under "the math screens experiments". |
| **Weather / climate** (§19) | Observational data; compute | Low | **High (proven)** | ML weather models are already in operational use, built on 40+ years of reanalysis data and clear scoring metrics; long-term climate prediction is still limited by our understanding of processes such as clouds and aerosols. |
| **Chemistry / materials** (§19) | Synthesis and characterization throughput; data quality; "synthesizability" | **Low** | Medium (has been overstated) | A computational prediction of "stable" does not mean novel, synthesizable or useful (17.3). |
| **Biology / medicine** (§20) | Mechanistic data; wet-lab throughput; clinical trials (7.9% from Phase I to approval); regulation | **Low** | **Medium–high** (but limited by Amdahl) | AlphaFold changed structural biology, but it did not shorten clinical trials. |
| **Neuroscience** (§20) | Measurement (too few neurons recorded at once, connectomes too expensive); "what to measure" | Low–medium | **Medium** | The whole-brain fruit fly connectome (FlyWire, *Nature* 2024, doi:10.1038/s41586-024-07558-y) was completed with ML image segmentation plus human proofreading, a typical case of "AI's other capabilities". The theory is a type B problem. |
| **AI / ML theory** (§21) | Type B: we don't know which mathematical statements matter for practice | **Medium** | **High** | Type A lemmas (such as the RLCT of specific architectures) will be proved in bulk, but deep learning theory has historically had little impact on practice; automated experiments and interpretability tools are more direct. |
| **Economics / social sciences** (§22) | Data for causal identification; the ethics and cost of human experiments; institutional adoption | Low (except mechanism design: medium) | Medium | Mechanism design is one of the few branches where "the math is the product" (case 10), but deployment is held up by legislation; the rest is held up by data and the social cost of experiments. |

**Three cross-cutting conclusions** [speculation]:

1. **The fields where "AI math" helps a lot all have an automatic judge** (proof checkers, tests, standards), and their deliverables are digital. These are exactly the fields with a large p in Amdahl's law.
2. **"AI's other capabilities" have proven effective in fields with plenty of data and clear scoring** (weather, protein structure); in fields with little data where acceptance depends on wet labs, they are often overstated (materials, automated labs).
3. **Every field eventually runs into the layer of "statement / spec / what to measure"** (type B). Proofs cannot solve this layer, and AI's other capabilities have not visibly sped it up either. It is the next bottleneck, and it is also where AI safety meets this topic: if the spec is wrong, the cheaper proofs get, the faster things go wrong.
