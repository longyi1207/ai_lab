# Computing, cryptography and quantum computing: rebuilding the trust stack once proofs are cheap

> **Tags**: [established] textbook-level or replicated by several groups; [recent] new results from 2024–2026; [speculation] inference; ⚠ could not be verified, or only secondary sources. **Capability curves** (see §17): ① METR time horizon, about 150 hours by mid-2027 in the central scenario; ② single-attempt solve rate on formalizable open problems, about 18% by end-2027 and about 45% by end-2028; ③ formal proof cost about $0.023/line, falling to 1/10 each year. **Type A**: clearly stated, missing a proof; **type B**: it isn't even clear what to prove.
>
> In computer science, the proof itself is the product: correctness proofs for compilers, security arguments for encryption standards and resource estimates for quantum circuits directly decide whether real systems can be used. So the first-order effect of "AI solving math" is much larger here than in biology or materials. But most real incidents still come from implementations, specs and institutions.

## 18.0 The bottom line

1. **The bottleneck in cybersecurity has shifted from "finding vulnerabilities" to "fixing them", and what drove the shift is AI's coding ability, not its math ability.** Anthropic's Glasswing program flagged 6,202 high-severity candidates; of the roughly 530 disclosed so far, only 75 have been patched (2026-05) [recent]. The only defense that can keep up is "proving that a class of vulnerabilities is absent"; proofs are getting cheaper, and the new bottlenecks are **whether the spec is right** and **whether the trusted computing base (TCB) has holes**.
2. **Cryptography is built on unproven conjectures, and AI math will audit them at scale.** Plenty of standards candidates have been broken by pure math: SIKE (2022, about an hour on a single core), Rainbow (2022, a weekend on a laptop), and the "proximity gaps" conjecture, which a batch of zkVMs relied on for their parameters and which was refuted in 2025 [established/recent]. The probability that ML-KEM suffers a practical classical break before 2030 is estimated at ≤5% [speculation]; young post-quantum signatures and ZK parameter conjectures carry much higher risk.
3. **Math is pulling Q-day earlier.** The number of physical qubits needed to break RSA-2048 fell from about 20 million in 2019 to under 100,000 in 2026 (qLDPC architecture) [recent]. Resource estimation is a type A problem with an automatic judge, and AI will keep pushing this line down; hardware is still the main bottleneck.
4. **"What can quantum computing do besides breaking cryptography and simulating quantum systems?" is where math is most purely the bottleneck.** Practical exponential speedups are rare, "dequantization" keeps knocking claimed speedups back down, and AI-driven classical simulation is competing with quantum computing for the same applications.
5. **"Faster algorithms" splits into two worlds.** Asymptotic records (ω pushed down to 2.371177 in 2026-08 with AlphaEvolve's help, and to 9/4 by OpenAI's claim in §6) will hardly make GPUs any faster. Practical speedups come from "AI writes code + search + benchmarks as the judge", for example AlphaEvolve continuously recovering 0.7% of Google's worldwide compute [established], and they rely on AI's other capabilities.
6. **Big problems like P vs NP are protected by "barrier theorems"; they are classic type B problems, and even a proof would barely change practice.** Only a few kinds of results would change the world: the unconditional existence of one-way functions, quantum algorithms for lattice problems, and impossibility theorems about "what can be audited efficiently" (for example, provably undetectable backdoors in models).
7. **Three connections to AI safety**: (a) the "box" around an AI agent is itself software, and verifiable isolation is the physical foundation of AI control; (b) ZK and FHE can "verify without revealing" and could serve as infrastructure for AI governance, but they are in tension with misuse monitoring; (c) cryptanalytic capability should be included in dangerous-capability evaluations, while mainstream frameworks currently seem to focus mainly on cyberattack operations ([speculation], not systematically verified).

## 18.2 Problem 1: Formal verification of critical software × cyber offense and defense in the AI era

**In one sentence**: AI makes "finding vulnerabilities" almost free, and the only defense that can keep up is "proving there are no vulnerabilities of this kind". Proofs are getting cheaper too, and the new bottlenecks become "what exactly is being proved (the spec)" and "what are we trusting (the TCB)".

**Background**: *Testing samples; proof covers every input.* Look at this C function:

```c
int abs(int x) { return x < 0 ? -x : x; }
```

Random testing on 1,000 inputs passes every time. But when `x = INT_MIN`, `-x` overflows and the function returns a negative number; the chance that a random test hits this is 1/2³² ≈ 2.3×10⁻¹⁰. Write the spec as "the return value is ≥ 0" and hand it to a proof checker, and the proof gets stuck on exactly this one input: either fix the code, or add the precondition "x ≠ INT_MIN" to the spec.

Formal verification has three ingredients: a spec, an implementation, and a proof checked by a very small "kernel" (Lean, Isabelle, Coq/Rocq; see §3). You only have to trust a kernel of a few thousand lines, not the person or AI who wrote the proof. *The hard part is the spec*: the spec for sorting should be "the output is sorted, and is a permutation of the input"; if you only write "the output is sorted", then "always return an empty list" also passes the proof. The cost used to be very high: the seL4 microkernel has about 8,700 lines of C with about 200,000 lines of hand-written proof, about 25 person-years [established].

**Why it matters**: offense has already industrialized. At the DARPA AIxCC finals (2025-08), automated systems found 54 of 63 planted vulnerabilities, at an average of about $152 per task [recent]. Defense cannot keep up: the Glasswing report says the work is now limited by how fast it can verify, disclose and patch (paraphrased) [recent]. For AI safety, an agent's "box" is software: Glasswing's examples include memory-corruption bugs in a hypervisor **written in a memory-safe language**, and a chain of 4 vulnerabilities used to escape a browser sandbox. AI control schemes assume the box is secure, and verifiable isolation is the foundation of that assumption.

**What's blocking it: is math the bottleneck?** Proof labor used to be the main bottleneck, and it is disappearing. By curve ③, seL4's 200,000 lines of proof come to about $4,600 in tokens, and about $200 by end-2027 [model]; 10 AI agents have autonomously written a Lean version of zlib (lean-zip) and proved that "compressing and then decompressing any data smaller than 1 GiB gives back the original data" [recent]. The new bottlenecks are not math: (1) **specs**: fuzzing lean-zip with its proofs stripped out, for about 100 million executions, found zero memory issues in the proved parts, but the archive parser, for which no theorem was written, had a denial-of-service bug, and **the Lean 4 runtime itself** had a heap overflow [recent]; (2) **the TCB and hardware models**: Spectre shows that "isolation" proved against an instruction-set spec cannot stop side channels that the spec does not describe [established]; (3) **legacy code and institutions**: billions of lines of C/C++ are maintained by volunteers, and the "dumb" fix of switching languages works enormously well: Rust code has about 0.2 memory-safety vulnerabilities per million lines, versus about 1,000 historically for C/C++ [recent]; (4) **performance**: according to secondary reports, the Lean Zstd decompressor that Adam Langley had a model prove runs at only about 1/10 the speed of standard zstd ⚠. Judgment: writing proofs is type A and is being conquered fast; writing specs, drawing the TCB boundary and driving adoption are type B problems plus institutional ones.

**What AI can do**: *AI math/proof*: write proofs against a spec; repair proofs automatically after code changes; prove old and new implementations equivalent (for example, C translated into Rust); the repository-level benchmark Vero also has agents **prove that a given spec is unsatisfiable**, which amounts to auditing specs by machine. *AI's other capabilities*: fuzzing, building exploits, writing patches; mining candidate specs from RFCs and tests; migrating C to Rust at scale.

**If solved: first-order impact**: cryptographic libraries, parsers, kernels, hypervisors and consensus protocols ship with proofs by default; memory-safety and parsing bugs in them drop to near zero, and the cost shifts to spec review. Distributed protocol models written in TLA+ can be proved to refine all the way down to the actual code.

**If solved: second-order impact**: (1) **The offense–defense balance follows a "worse before better" curve** [speculation]: 2026–2028 favors attackers, with patching at human speed; once critical components are proved, the attack surface moves to spec errors, configuration, the supply chain, social engineering, hardware side channels and ML components that cannot be verified. (2) **Software liability becomes law**: the EU Cyber Resilience Act (Regulation (EU) 2024/2847) has already introduced security liability for digital products [established], and "is there a proof" may become a due-diligence standard [speculation]. (3) **AI safety**: an AI that writes proofs can also write **specs**, and a spec that looks reasonable but hides a backdoor is very hard for humans to spot, so "who reviews the specs" becomes an alignment problem; meanwhile proof checkers (with fixed statements, and `sorry` and new axioms banned) are **reward signals that are very hard to reward-hack**, which will speed up AI coding ability, so they are dual-use in their own right.

**Timing**: Optimistic: by mid-2027 (curve ① at about 150 hours, roughly one work-month) AI can autonomously translate a C library of 10,000–50,000 lines into a version with proofs. Central: in 2027–2028 proof labor stops being the main cost (curve ③), while spec review still runs at human speed; by 2030 proved code covers only "critical small kernels", under 1% of all code [speculation]; the window favoring attackers lasts until about 2028. Pessimistic: specs, performance and maintainer bandwidth all get stuck at once, and in 2030 this is still only an internal practice at a few big companies.

**Counterarguments and uncertainty**: most intrusions come from credentials, phishing and misconfiguration (Verizon DBIR 2024 says about 68% involve "the human element" ⚠ from memory), and proofs do not help there. Glasswing's numbers come mainly from Anthropic's own reports, with limited independent evaluation.

**Signals to watch**: patch rates in later Glasswing reports; the share of memory-safety bugs among CVEs; core open-source projects requiring "PRs with proofs"; the first public incident of "a backdoor in the spec itself".

## 18.3 Problem 2: Post-quantum lattice cryptography (ML-KEM) — will AI find a break, or build confidence?

**In one sentence**: the internet is moving key exchange to ML-KEM, whose security rests on the **conjecture** that "the Learning With Errors problem (LWE) is hard". AI math will audit it at an unprecedented scale, and the outcome could be a break (catastrophic, but with hybrid schemes as a backstop), higher confidence, or a small reduction in security bits.

**Background**: Shor's quantum algorithm can quickly break RSA and elliptic-curve cryptography (ECC), so new hard problems are needed. *Lattices*: in the plane, take the basis b₁=(2,0), b₂=(1,2); all integer combinations form a regular grid of points. The same lattice can also be described by a "bad" basis, for example b₁ and b₂'=(11,2) (= b₂ + 5·b₁): exactly the same points, but the two vectors are long and almost parallel. **The shortest vector problem (SVP)**: given only the bad basis, find the shortest nonzero lattice vector. In two dimensions this is fast; in a few hundred dimensions, the best known algorithms all take exponential time. A good basis is the "trapdoor".

*LWE is a system of linear equations with noise.* Working mod 17, let the secret be s = (3, 5):

| a | a·s mod 17 | noise e | public value b |
|---|---|---|---|
| (1, 2) | 13 | +1 | 14 |
| (4, 1) | 0 | −1 | 16 |
| (2, 7) | 7 | 0 | 7 |

Without noise, Gaussian elimination solves for s immediately; with ±1 noise, every equation is "slightly off". In two dimensions you can simply enumerate the 17² = 289 candidates, but ML-KEM-768 has effective dimension 768 and modulus 3329 (FIPS 203), about 182 bits of classical security by the Core-SVP estimate ⚠ (from memory). **The security of the whole scheme rests on that little bit of noise.** Regev (2005) proved that "solving random LWE ⇒ solving worst-case lattice problems", but the reduction is not tight and cannot fix concrete parameters; parameters are set by "how much the best known attack costs".

*Past breaks are the most important background for this card.* **SIKE** (a NIST round-4 candidate) was broken in 2022-07 by Castryck–Decru using a pure-math theorem from 1997, in about an hour on a single core [established]. **Rainbow** (a round-3 finalist signature scheme): the title of Beullens's 2022 paper is literally "Breaking Rainbow Takes a Weekend on a Laptop" [established]. **Chen 2024** claimed a polynomial-time quantum algorithm for LWE; about a week later an error was found and the claim was withdrawn [established]. A **Luo 2026** preprint claims to break ML-KEM; it has been informally shown to get the dimensions wrong, and **does not currently constitute a break** ⚠.

**Why it matters**: by late 2025-10, more than half of the human TLS traffic Cloudflare received already used the hybrid key exchange X25519MLKEM768 [recent]. "Harvest now, decrypt later" means that for data that must stay secret for 10–30 years, the question of whether ML-KEM is secure has to be answered **now**. Signatures are harder: switching entire certificate chains to ML-DSA-44 adds about 15 kB to a TLS handshake, and the smaller schemes rest on **younger, less-audited math**, which is exactly where AI cryptanalysis is most likely to make its first impact.

**What's blocking it: is math the bottleneck?** For **confidence**, math is the bottleneck: nobody can prove LWE is hard, confidence comes only from "many smart people have tried and failed", and expert attention is scarce. For **real incidents**, the bottleneck is implementation: KyberSlash found that several implementations, including the official reference code, had secret-dependent division timing, enough to "reliably recover the key within minutes" [established]. For **migration speed**, the bottleneck is engineering and institutions: in 2025 only 3.7% of Cloudflare's origin servers supported post-quantum key exchange.

**What AI can do**: *AI math/proof*: systematically extend known attack families (for example, pushing attacks on rank-1 ideal lattices toward rank-2 and rank-3 module lattices), a well-defined direction and type A; act as a judge at scale, formalizing claimed breaks like Chen's and Luo's and finding the errors within days; audit the attack-cost estimates themselves (Ducas–Pulles 2023 showed problems with the heuristic assumptions behind dual attacks [established]). *AI's other capabilities*: Meta's SALSA series uses Transformers to recover LWE secrets, but it only works for sparse or very small secrets, not ML-KEM's parameters [recent]; checking implementations for constant-time behavior at scale.

**If solved: first-order impact**: *Scenario A (low probability)*: AI finds a practical classical break of ML-KEM. Hybrid schemes fall back to X25519's security, but all "harvest now, decrypt later" traffic becomes exposed to future quantum attackers; ML-KEM-only deployments (such as the CNSA 2.0 path ⚠) take a catastrophic hit, and the world has to switch urgently to code-based schemes (in 2025-03 NIST chose HQC as a backup). *Scenario B*: the search comes up empty, confidence rises, and the default parameters might even drop from 768 to 512. *Scenario C (most likely)*: each paper shaves off a few bits, and together they force parameters upward.

**If solved: second-order impact**: fewer and fewer human experts can check AI cryptanalysis, so trust in standards will rely more and more on **machine-checkable arguments**. States with private breaking capabilities will not disclose them, so "how to disclose dangerous mathematical results" needs new norms (problem 3). On AI safety: cryptanalytic capability should become a category of dangerous-capability evaluation [speculation]; protecting model weights presupposes cryptography, so if cryptography fails, the chain of trust in AI governance breaks at the very bottom; a flood of wrong AI-generated "breaks" would amount to a "denial-of-service attack" on expert attention.

**Timing**: papers in which AI helps shave off security bits (type A, with a clear objective function, following curve ②) become common from **2027** in the central scenario. At least one NIST additional signature candidate significantly weakened or broken thanks to AI-assisted analysis: central **2027–2029** [speculation]. A practical classical break of ML-KEM-768: **≤5% by 2030** [speculation]; "is LWE hard" is a research program rather than a short proof, so it is type B and outside what curve ② covers.

**Counterarguments and uncertainty**: lattice problems have been studied for more than 40 years counting from LLL (1982), and AI may only carry out old ideas faster. Bernstein (a strong advocate of hybrid schemes), on the other hand, argues that the risk is systematically underestimated, because "known attacks" are only a small part of the attack surface.

**Signals to watch**: cryptanalysis papers on ePrint that acknowledge AI; downward revisions to the lattice-estimator's estimates; frontier AI companies adding "cryptanalytic uplift" evaluations to their safety frameworks.

## 18.4 Problem 3: The Q-day countdown — how many qubits does it take to break RSA/ECC?

**In one sentence**: Q-day depends on two numbers: how fast hardware improves, and "how many qubits and how many gates a break takes". The second number is set entirely by math (algorithms, error-correcting codes, architecture), and it fell about 200-fold in 7 years.

**Background**: physical qubits make an error about 0.1% of the time per gate, and breaking RSA-2048 takes billions of gates, so the error rate per step has to be pushed below about 10⁻¹². The way to do this is **error correction**: encode one "logical qubit" in many physical qubits. The classical analogy is the repetition code: send each bit three times and take a majority vote; if a single copy flips with probability 1%, the vote is wrong about 3p² = 0.03% of the time. The most common quantum version is the **surface code**: on a d×d grid you repeatedly measure "syndromes", and a classical computer infers in real time where errors occurred (**decoding**). Each time the distance d increases by 2, the logical error rate is divided by a **suppression factor Λ**.

*An order-of-magnitude calculation by hand.* Google Willow has Λ = 2.14 [recent]. At that rate, pushing errors down another 10⁹-fold takes log(10⁹)/log(2.14) ≈ 27 steps of "+2", i.e. d ≈ 61, or about 2d² ≈ 7,400 physical qubits per logical qubit; with Λ = 10 it takes only 9 steps, d ≈ 25, about 1,250. The "uniform 0.1% gate error" commonly used in resource estimates corresponds roughly to Λ ≈ 10 [estimate], so **today's hardware is still about 5 times short of this assumption.** Better codes (qLDPC) save a lot: IBM's "gross code" protects 12 logical qubits with 288 physical qubits, where the surface code would need nearly 3,000 [established].

| Year | Target | Physical qubits | Time | Source |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2019 | RSA-2048 | ~20 million | ~8 hours | Gidney & Ekerå |
| 2025 | RSA-2048 | <1 million | <1 week | Gidney, same hardware assumptions |
| 2026-02 | RSA-2048 | <100,000 | Not given in the abstract | Pinnacle architecture (qLDPC) |
| 2026-03 | ECC P-256 | <500,000 (superconducting) | A few minutes | Google: <1,200 logical qubits, about 90 million Toffoli gates |
| 2026-03 | ECC P-256 | ~10,000–26,000 (neutral atoms) | A few days | Cain et al. |

The 2025 drop came **entirely** from algorithms and architecture (approximate residue arithmetic, "yoked" surface codes, "magic state cultivation") [recent]. **Elliptic curves are exposed earlier than RSA**, and TLS signatures, Bitcoin and Ethereum all use ECC.

**Why it matters**: Mosca's inequality: if data must stay secret for x years, migration takes y years, and Q-day is z years away, then **as soon as x + y > z, it is already too late.** When Google published its ECC estimate, it set its own migration target at 2029, and pointed out that a fast-clocked superconducting machine could compute the private key from a public key exposed in the mempool before a Bitcoin transaction is confirmed [recent].

**What's blocking it: is math the bottleneck?** **The main bottleneck is hardware; math is a multiplier.** "100,000 qubits running stably for days at a 0.1% error rate, with real-time decoding at microsecond scale" is still far from achieved. IBM plans to deliver Starling in 2029 (200 logical qubits, 100 million logical operations); ECC-256 needs about 100 million Toffoli gates and about 1,200 logical qubits, so **the gate count is already in the same range, while logical qubits are still about 6 times short** [estimate]. **Decoding is an underrated bottleneck**: AlphaQubit is more accurate, but Google acknowledges it is still too slow to keep up in real time [established]. How big is the multiplier [speculation]? If the number of usable qubits doubles every year ⚠ (a rough assumption), each 10-fold drop in resource requirements brings Q-day about 3.3 years closer.

**What AI can do**: *AI math/proof*: resource estimation is classic type A, with a clear objective function (Toffoli count, logical qubit count) and an automatic judge (stabilizer simulators): searching for new, implementable qLDPC code families, optimizing modular arithmetic circuits, designing cheaper magic-state protocols. AlphaEvolve-style "search + evaluator" is a perfect fit. *AI's other capabilities*: neural-network decoders, automatic calibration and chip design, which directly ease the hardware bottleneck.

**If solved: first-order impact**: Q-day estimates move earlier and their ranges narrow, giving policymakers harder numbers; fault-tolerant machines also become usable earlier for chemistry and materials simulation (§19).

**If solved: second-order impact**: *"Algorithmic Q-day"*: the date may be set by a paper rather than by a machine, so migration plans should be stress-tested against "resources falling another 10-fold before 2030" [speculation]. *Disclosure norms for dangerous math*: Google did not publish its ECC attack circuit; instead it attached a zero-knowledge proof generated with the SP1 zkVM + Groth16, letting third parties confirm that "such a circuit exists and the resource counts are correct"; ironically, the curve Groth16 uses is itself vulnerable to quantum attack [recent]. This template could carry over to other dangerous results that AI discovers, such as exploit chains and biological sequences. *Cryptocurrency governance*: whether "dormant coins" with exposed public keys should be frozen or left alone.

**Timing**: resource estimates (type A) fall another 2–10× before 2028 in the central scenario [speculation] (curve ②). A machine that can break ECC-256 [speculation]: optimistic 2030–2032, central 2033–2036, pessimistic after 2040. The basis: IBM's 2029 roadmap is in roughly the same range as the ECC resource requirements, and Google's 2029 migration target implies a risk assessment; this item depends mostly on hardware and is only weakly tied to the capability curves. RSA-2048 comes a few years later (secondary ⚠).

**Counterarguments and uncertainty**: hardware may get stuck on cooling, wiring, yield and correlated noise (cosmic rays, leakage), whereas resource estimates all assume noise is "uniform and independent". A few researchers (such as Gil Kalai) argue that noise fundamentally prevents scaling up; this has not been refuted by experiment.

**Signals to watch**: Λ ≥ 4; real-time decoding at distance ≥ 11; more than 1,000 logical qubits; resource estimates falling another 10-fold. One warning sign: a major company suddenly stopping publication of resource estimates.

## 18.5 Problem 4: New quantum algorithms and provable quantum advantage (short card)

Quantum speedups come from **interference** of amplitudes, like noise-canceling headphones: the waves of wrong answers cancel each other out. This requires the problem to have **structure** (such as the periodicity Shor exploits); unstructured search gets only Grover's quadratic speedup (10¹² steps become 10⁶), and every logical step carries error-correction overhead, so quadratic speedups will mostly be useless in the fault-tolerant era [established]. Math can also destroy speedups: in 2018, 18-year-old Ewin Tang used a classical algorithm to knock the exponential speedup of a quantum recommendation system back down ("dequantization") [established]; Lee et al. concluded that evidence for an exponential quantum advantage in ground-state energy calculation has yet to be found across chemical space (paraphrased) [established].

**Math is the bottleneck, in its purest form in this field, and mostly type B**: it isn't even clear "which class of problems to look in", and classical baselines (tensor networks, neural-network quantum states) keep raising the bar. AI math is already involved: Aaronson and Witteveen (2025) proved limits on black-box amplification for QMA, and a key step was a function proposed by GPT-5 [recent]. The counterintuitive part comes from AI's other capabilities: AI-driven classical simulation is taking over quantum computing's chemistry and materials applications, so the stronger AI gets, the fewer "killer apps" quantum computing may have [speculation] (§19).

Timing: concrete type A proofs are happening now; a whole new family of exponential speedups found mainly by AI: about 15–30% before 2030 [speculation], and curve ② does not apply. **An efficient quantum algorithm for lattice problems (LWE): <5% before 2030 [speculation], but with the highest impact weight**: if it happens, the entire post-quantum migration of problem 2 has to start over. **Sources**: Tang arXiv:1807.04271; quantum chemistry arXiv:2208.02199; Aaronson & Witteveen arXiv:2509.21131.

## 18.6 Problem 5: FHE and zero-knowledge proofs — making "computing on ciphertext" and "verifiable computation" cheap

**In one sentence**: fully homomorphic encryption (FHE) can compute on data without decrypting it, and zero-knowledge proofs (ZK) can prove "the computation was done correctly" without revealing the inputs. Both already exist mathematically, but their overhead is thousands to tens of millions of times that of plaintext computation (order-of-magnitude estimate ⚠), and efficiency depends half on math and half on engineering. They are the most promising tools for "verifiable AI governance".

**Background**: *FHE is like a glove box*: you work through the walls of the box without seeing inside. A toy example: Enc(m) = m + k, so Enc(3) + Enc(4) = 7 + 2k; whoever holds k subtracts 2k to get 7, and the server has done the addition without knowing 3 or 4. Real FHE is built on LWE, **the same hard problem ML-KEM relies on**; operations make the noise grow, so it has to be refreshed periodically by "bootstrapping", the most expensive step. *ZK is like a random spot check*: the prover encodes the computation as polynomials. Two different polynomials of degree d agree on at most d points (the Schwartz–Zippel lemma), so the verifier picks one random point in a field of size 2⁶⁴, and the chance of being fooled is at most d/2⁶⁴; for d = 10⁶ that is about 5×10⁻¹⁴.

*Soundness depends on a conjecture.* FRI-type protocols rely on "**proximity gaps**" for Reed–Solomon codes: if even one vector in a batch is far from every valid codeword, then a random linear combination of the batch is almost always far as well. Many systems set their parameters by the **conjecture** that "this property holds all the way up to the capacity limit", in exchange for smaller proofs. In 2025-11, Crites and Stewart proved that conjectures of this kind **are false** [recent]; according to community tallies, around twenty zkVMs had assumed it ⚠.

**Why it matters**: SP1 Hypercube used about 160 RTX 4090s to prove 93% of Ethereum mainnet blocks within 12 seconds, with progress coming mainly from engineering; zkLLM can produce a proof under 200 kB for one inference of a 13-billion-parameter model in 15 minutes [recent]. Apple already uses FHE for private queries [established].

**What's blocking it: is math the bottleneck?** Half and half. Math sets parameter sizes (soundness theorems) and brings new protocols and cheaper bootstrapping; engineering handles hardware acceleration and memory bandwidth. There is also a spec problem: a SoK analyzed 141 real SNARK vulnerabilities, and many came from "underconstrained" circuits ⚠, which has the same structure as problem 1.

**What AI can do**: *AI math/proof*: proximity gaps are a type A problem that is **clearly stated and has an automatic judge**. In 2026-08 the Ethereum Foundation launched the better.codes challenge: the problems are formalized in Lean 4, the goal is to push the provable soundness lower bound to 128 bits, and it explicitly invites people to "bring your own AI agent" [recent]. This is the cleanest test bed for "AI math directly changing industrial parameters". *AI's other capabilities*: writing GPU kernels, compiling programs into circuits, checking whether constraints are complete.

**If solved: first-order impact**: with general-purpose ZK overhead down to 10–100×, you get a "verifiable cloud"; with FHE down to 10–100× on specific workloads, medical and financial data can be used for inference without leaving its home domain.

**If solved: second-order impact**: *Verifiable AI governance* [speculation]: proving that "this output came from the model whose weights hash to H", that "the model passed evaluation set S" (without revealing the weights), or that "inference really did go through the safety filter" follows the same logic as arms-control verification: **verify, without disclosing**; proving "how much compute was used in training" is much harder, and there is no workable scheme yet. *The tension between privacy and oversight*: private inference with FHE means the provider **cannot see** what users are asking, so misuse monitoring stops working; the more private it is, the harder misuse is to prevent [speculation]. *Mathematical risk in finance*: billions of dollars of rollup assets rest partly on unproven coding-theory conjectures, and AI will turn "conjecture-driven parameters" into "proof-driven parameters".

**Timing**: provable bounds on proximity gaps, or better.codes reaching 128 bits: type A with a Lean judge, substantive progress in 2027–2028 in the central scenario (curve ②) [speculation]. Real-time proving of Ethereum blocks: already essentially achieved in engineering terms. FHE inference overhead for frontier-scale LLMs falling below 100×: after 2030 in the central scenario [speculation], depending on hardware and largely unrelated to AI math.

**Counterarguments and uncertainty**: trusted execution environments (TEEs, such as the H100's confidential computing mode) are far cheaper and may take over most uses of FHE/ZK, at the cost of trusting the hardware vendor and accepting side-channel risk.

**Signals to watch**: provable security bits on the better.codes leaderboard; whether frontier-scale models can produce inference proofs; whether regulatory documents list "cryptographic proofs" as compliance evidence.

**Sources**: ePrint 2025/2046, 2026/782 (proximity gap counterexamples); better.codes https://blog.ethereum.org/2026/08/20/better-codes-challenge ; SP1 Hypercube https://blog.succinct.xyz/sp1-hypercube/ ; Apple HE https://machinelearning.apple.com/research/homomorphic-encryption ; arXiv:2404.16109 (zkLLM), 2402.15293 (SoK on SNARK vulnerabilities).

## 18.7 Problem 6: Algorithm discovery — asymptotic records vs real speedups on GPUs (short card)

Big-O notation hides constants; an algorithm that is asymptotically faster but has constants of cosmic size is called a "galactic algorithm". The matrix multiplication exponent ω fell to **2.371177** in 2026-08 with AlphaEvolve's help [recent]. Working out by hand what that is worth: the exponent dropped by 0.000162, and at n = 10⁵, n^0.000162 ≈ 1.0019, a saving of only 0.19% of operations; and laser-method algorithms cannot be implemented in practice anyway. OpenAI's claimed ω ≤ 9/4 in §6 is a much bigger step, but it too is an existence proof and a galactic algorithm, and §6.10.2 already explained that it has no direct impact on GPUs or LLM training: what GPUs run on their tensor cores is still the n³ algorithm. What is close to practical are decompositions for **fixed small sizes** (AlphaEvolve's 48 multiplications for 4×4 complex matrices; a follow-up paper tries to "bring it into practice", but its abstract reports no measured speedup). Shortest paths broke the sorting barrier for the first time in 2025, but at n = 10⁹ the new algorithm is in theory only about 3 times faster than Dijkstra, a gain that constants and caches will likely eat up [recent].

**For asymptotic records the bottleneck is math; for practical speedups it is not**: there the bottleneck is understanding the hardware (memory hierarchy, bandwidth), reliable benchmarking and correctness checking. Practical speedups come from AI's other capabilities, "write code + evolutionary search + benchmarks as the judge": one Borg scheduling rule from AlphaEvolve continuously recovers 0.7% of Google's worldwide compute, and one matrix multiplication kernel for Gemini got 23% faster, cutting training time by about 1% [established]. High-performance code written by AI risks gaming the evaluation (related incidents in 2025 ⚠ from memory), so the single most useful thing AI math can do here is **prove that an optimized kernel is equivalent to the reference implementation**.

Second-order impact: making Gemini training 1% faster is **AI optimizing AI's own training stack**, the plainest form of recursive self-improvement, and one that has already happened; progress in algorithmic efficiency will weaken regulation that uses compute as its threshold [speculation] (§22). Timing: kernel-level optimization is already happening; tasks at the level of compilers or scheduling systems become feasible around 2027 in the central scenario (curve ①); asymptotic records will see many small AI-assisted improvements in 2026–2028 (curve ②). **Sources**: AlphaEvolve https://deepmind.google/discover/blog/alphaevolve-a-gemini-powered-coding-agent-for-designing-advanced-algorithms/ ; arXiv:2608.16884 (ω < 2.371177), 2506.13242 and 2609.12027 (4×4), 2504.17033 (shortest paths).

## 18.8 Problem 7: Complexity theory — which proofs would change the world, and which would not

**In one sentence**: big problems like P vs NP are blocked by "barrier theorems" and are type B; AI can already produce type A results in complexity theory. A few target theorems would change the foundations of cryptography and AI safety; most would change no practice at all.

**Background**: P is the class of problems that can be **solved** quickly; NP is the class whose solutions can be **verified** quickly (Sudoku: checking a filled-in grid is easy, but solving the generalization to n²×n² grids is NP-complete). *Why proving P≠NP would not help practice*: P≠NP means "hard **in the worst case**", while cryptography needs "hard **on average**" plus the existence of one-way functions. To use a lock analogy: worst-case hard means "there exist some people who can't open this lock"; average-case hard means "anyone you pick at random can't open it", and only the latter protects data. Liu and Pass (2020) proved that one-way functions exist **if and only if** time-bounded Kolmogorov complexity is somewhat hard on average [established].

*Barriers.* Three theorems, relativization, natural proofs (Razborov–Rudich 1997) and algebrization, prove that certain classes of techniques can never prove P≠NP [established]. The most elegant is natural proofs: **if strong pseudorandom functions exist (that is, if cryptography is possible), "natural" methods cannot prove circuit lower bounds**; the very existence of cryptography is the reason proving hardness is so hard. For explicit Boolean functions, the best known circuit lower bound is only about 3.1n (linear), while the true lower bound is believed to be exponential ⚠.

**Why it matters**: the practical impact of different results varies enormously:

| Result | Practical impact |
|---|---|
| Proving P ≠ NP | Almost none: it confirms what is already believed; the value is in the new techniques |
| P = NP with a practical algorithm | Civilization-scale, but extremely unlikely |
| Unconditional existence of one-way functions | Cryptography gets an unconditional foundation; actual schemes still rely on specific assumptions |
| BPP = P; proving or refuting the UGC | Small: clarifies which approximation algorithms are optimal (UGC: see §7) |
| Impossibility theorems about "what can be audited efficiently" | **Large impact on AI safety** |

An example for the last row: Goldwasser et al. (2022) proved that backdoors can be planted in models that **no computationally bounded observer can detect**: a construction based on digital signatures is undetectable in the black-box setting, and for models such as random ReLU networks it is undetectable even in the white-box setting [established]. So **inspecting the weights alone cannot, in principle, rule out all backdoors**, and safety guarantees must rest partly on provenance proofs for the training process (linking back to problem 5). Another root: the theoretical motivation for debate (AI safety via debate) is exactly IP = PSPACE, i.e. a weak verifier can check a strong prover through interaction; characterizing "when computationally bounded human judges remain reliable" amounts to drawing the boundary of scalable oversight (§21).

**What's blocking it: is math the bottleneck?** It is pure math, and the big problems are type B: known techniques have been proven insufficient, and entirely new ideas are needed. Compute helps only when searching over finite structures (gadgets, small circuits).

**What AI can do**: *AI math/proof*: it has already produced type A results. Nagda, Raghavan and Thakurta (2025) used AlphaEvolve to construct gadgets and proved that "approximating MAX-4-CUT to within 0.987 is NP-hard" (previous record 0.9883) [recent]. AI can also formalize complexity theory (the complexity library in Lean is very thin, an infrastructure gap). *AI's other capabilities*: exhaustively finding minimal circuits for small functions, generating conjectures.

**If solved: first-order impact**: if AI finds new techniques that get around the barriers, they will almost certainly spill over into cryptography (new constructions or new attacks) and learning theory.

**If solved: second-order impact**: complexity theory will become a language for **understanding AI itself**. "Verifying is easier than generating" is the foundation of the whole safety paradigm of debate, scalable oversight, and "AI writes the proof, the kernel checks it"; once a theorem reveals cases where verification is also hard, these schemes will have to be redesigned. Impossibility theorems (backdoors are undetectable, watermarks are removable) will push AI governance from "detection" toward "provenance proofs and process control".

**Timing**: gadgets and approximation bounds (type A) are happening now and will appear in large numbers in 2026–2028 (curve ②). New impossibility/possibility theorems about AI auditing and verification: in the central scenario, a batch of them proved with AI involvement in 2027–2029 [speculation]. P vs NP, and the unconditional existence of one-way functions: each below 2% before 2035 [speculation]; they are not in the pool of "formalizable problems of known shape" that curve ② covers.

**Counterarguments and uncertainty**: AI might find "alien" techniques that humans would not think of, and the barriers rule out only **certain classes** of techniques; it is also possible that these big problems are independent of the standard axioms.

**Signals to watch**: the share of FOCS/STOC papers that acknowledge AI; the growth of Lean's complexity library; circuit lower bounds for explicit functions breaking past linear.

**Sources**: arXiv:2509.18057 (Nagda et al.), 2009.11514 (Liu–Pass), 2204.06974 (undetectable backdoors), 1805.00899 (debate); Razborov & Rudich, JCSS 1997.

## 18.9 Second-order effects in this field

**1. "Proofs are cheap, statements are expensive" recurs across all seven problems, just under a different name each time.**

| Problem | The cheap part (type A) | The expensive part (type B) |
|---|---|---|
| 1 Formal verification | Writing proofs | Writing specs, drawing the TCB boundary |
| 2 Lattice cryptography | Improving existing attacks, formalizing reductions | What the security model leaves out (timing, implementation) |
| 3 Q-day | Optimizing circuits and codes | Hardware, and realistic noise models |
| 4 Quantum algorithms | Proving lower bounds, checking specific speedups | Which class of problems to look in |
| 5 FHE/ZK | Proving or refuting proximity gaps | Whether the circuit constraints are complete |
| 6 Algorithms | Improving asymptotic bounds | What counts as "really fast" |
| 7 Complexity | Constructing gadgets | New ideas that get around the barriers |

**2. Asymmetric speeds are the most dangerous dynamic in this field.** Finding vulnerabilities is fast, fixing them is slow; discovering attacks is fast, migrating standards is slow; AI generating "break claims" is fast, expert checking is slow; resource estimates fall fast, hardware moves slowly. So 2026–2029 is likely to be a **fragile transitional window** [speculation]: attackers reap AI's dividends first, while defenders' dividends (verified rewrites, post-quantum migration, proof-driven parameters) have to wait for institutions to catch up.

**3. The trust stack will be rebuilt on machine-checkable artifacts.** At the bottom are verified cryptographic libraries and kernels, in the middle post-quantum key exchange and smaller signatures, and at the top ZK-proved computation. Every layer has a "who checks the checker" problem: the Lean runtime bug in lean-zip and Groth16's own vulnerability to quantum attack are both reminders.

**4. Mathematical results are starting to need "responsible disclosure".** Google disclosed a quantum attack estimate with a ZK proof; Anthropic first publishes hashes of vulnerability details and releases the details only after fixes. These two practices will become standard moves when AI discovers dangerous results [speculation].

**5. Summary of the connections to AI safety.** Verifiable sandboxes (problem 1); cryptanalysis and vulnerability discovery included in dangerous-capability evaluations (problems 1, 2); governance tools that "verify without disclosing", and their tension with misuse monitoring (problem 5); undetectable backdoors putting a ceiling on model auditing (problem 7); algorithmic efficiency weakening regulation that uses compute as its threshold (problem 6); proof checkers as reward signals that are hard to hack, speeding up AI coding ability (problem 1).

**6. Connections to other fields.** Quantum computing and AI-driven classical simulation compete for the same applications (§19); the security of cryptocurrencies depends on coding-theory conjectures and on how long ECC lasts; software liability is moving into law (§22); private cryptanalytic and quantum capabilities are asymmetric weapons in geopolitics. An analogy from neuroscience [speculation, not a strict correspondence]: the brain uses slower prefrontal control to constrain fast association; computer security in the AI era is also moving toward "a powerful but untrusted generator + a small, trusted checker".
