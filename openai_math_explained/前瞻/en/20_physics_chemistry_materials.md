# Physics, chemistry and materials: the equations are known; the hard part is computing, measuring and building

> Tags: [established] accepted results; [recent] new results from 2024–2026; [speculation] inference; ⚠ unverified or disputed. Timing calls tie into the three curves of §18: **curve ①** METR task length (central: about 150 h by mid-2027, about 400 h by end of 2027); **curve ②** single-attempt solve rate on formalizable open problems (central: about 18% by end of 2027, about 45% by end of 2028, about 77% by end of 2030); **curve ③** cost of formal proof (falling about 10× per year). **Type A** = statement clear, proof missing; **type B** = not even clear what to prove. Problems 4, 6 and 8 are short cards.

## 20.0 The bottom line

- **Most of the equations have long been known.** In 1929 Dirac said that the physical laws needed for chemistry are "completely known", and the only trouble is that the equations can't be solved [established]. The hard parts are: **can't compute** (the cost of the many-electron equation grows exponentially), **can't measure precisely** (synthesis and characterization are ambiguous), and **can't build** (getting from the lab to engineering). So AI math acts here mainly through **algorithms**, not through **theorems**.
- **Only three kinds of math can change the direction of physics**: impossibility theorems (e.g. Schuch–Verstraete for DFT), existence results and counterexamples (e.g. the 2026-09 counterexample to Grad's conjecture), and computer-assisted proofs (e.g. Euler blowup). Most of the rest are **model-health proofs**, which confirm what physicists already believed.
- **The type A list in mathematical physics is being cleared in bulk.** This batch of OpenAI manuscripts contains about 25 mathematical-physics families (secondhand count ⚠). They claim to have solved Anderson extended states, spontaneous magnetization of the Heisenberg ferromagnet, the Haldane gap and hard-sphere BEC, plus the forced NS of §11 and the Vlasov–Maxwell of §12. None has been reviewed. Judgment [speculation]: problems of this kind will be mostly swept up in 2027–2028, yet they will change almost no experiment or engineering.
- **The high-impact problems are stuck outside mathematics**: electronic structure, high-temperature superconductivity, turbulence closure, fusion control, cloud feedback. What helps is AI's **other capabilities**: neural network functionals, ML interatomic potentials, differentiable simulators, reinforcement learning control (tested on the TCV and DIII-D devices), learned weather models (ECMWF's AIFS went operational in 2025-02), and autonomous labs.
- **Candidates are cheap and verification is expensive; how fast AI speeds a field up depends on how fast its referee is.** GNoME predicted about 380,000 stable crystals, and outside groups have synthesized only 736. This is the same bottleneck shift as in mathematics, where "proofs are cheap, auditing statements is expensive". Weather is scored every day and has been rewritten by AI; climate is scored once every few decades and moves slowly.
- **Energy impact arrives slowly.** Going from lab to grid usually takes 10–20 years. Before 2030 the only things likely to land are: weather forecasting (already happened), incremental improvement of batteries and catalysts, and faster control and design of fusion devices.
- **For AI safety**: physics and chemistry have automatic referees, so they will be where autonomous AI research gets going first and where AI first gets to act in the physical world. They also form a feedback loop, "AI → energy, chips, materials → more compute". Chemistry's dual-use risk is the most direct. There are ready-made examples of specification gaming (forced NS; DFT functionals whose energies fit better and better while their densities drift further and further off).

## 20.2 Problem 1: Electronic structure (the many-electron Schrödinger equation and DFT)

**In one sentence** All the "rules" of chemistry and materials are written in one equation, but the cost of solving it exactly grows exponentially with the number of electrons. The whole industry runs on an approximation (density functional theory, DFT), and nobody knows the exact form of its central term.

**Background** A **Hamiltonian** is "the recipe for total energy": electron kinetic energy + attraction by the nuclei + repulsion between electrons. The **Schrödinger equation** HΨ = EΨ asks for a **wave function** Ψ such that applying the recipe to it is the same as "multiplying by a number E"; the smallest E is the ground-state energy. Ψ assigns a number to "every combination of all the electron positions". Almost everything chemistry cares about is an **energy difference**: heat of reaction, reaction barrier, binding strength, battery voltage.

*Exponential explosion (by hand).* With 10 grid points per coordinate, 1 electron needs 10³ numbers and N electrons need 10^(3N). Benzene has 42 electrons, which means 10^126 numbers, while the universe contains only about 10^80 atoms. Switching to the orbital basis sets that chemists use, the exponential is still there: the 54-electron, 54-orbital "active space" commonly used for FeMoco, the active site of nitrogenase, has about 3.8×10³⁰ configurations, which no classical computer can handle.

*DFT's trick.* The Hohenberg–Kohn theorem (1964) proves that the ground-state energy depends only on the **electron density** n(r), which has just 3 variables [established]. Kohn–Sham (1965) rewrote the problem in terms of "fictitious non-interacting electrons", bringing the cost down to about N³. The price is that all the hard many-body effects get swept into one term: the **exchange-correlation functional** E_xc[n]. The theorem guarantees that it exists but does not say what it looks like, hence a ladder of approximations and hundreds of functionals.

*Why 1 kcal/mol matters (by hand).* A reaction rate is roughly proportional to exp(−barrier/RT), and at 300 K, RT ≈ 0.596 kcal/mol. An error of 1.37 kcal/mol in the barrier changes the rate by 10×; common DFT barrier errors are 3–5 kcal/mol, and at 4 the rate is off by about 820×. That is why 1 kcal/mol is called "chemical accuracy". The gold standard, CCSD(T), reaches it, but its cost grows as N⁷: double the size of the molecule and the computation grows 128×.

**Why it matters** Haber–Bosch ammonia synthesis uses about 1–2% of global energy (IEA), and about half the world's population depends on synthetic nitrogen fertilizer for its food (Erisman et al. 2008) [established]. Nitrogenase fixes nitrogen at room temperature, but the mechanism of FeMoco still has not been worked out computationally; it is also the flagship case for "what chemistry can quantum computers solve" (Reiher et al. 2017). The key quantities for batteries, catalysts and drugs are also energy differences on the order of 1 kcal/mol.

**What's blocking it: is math the bottleneck?** Theorems are not the bottleneck; **approximation algorithms and data** are. One "impossibility" theorem fixes the direction: Schuch & Verstraete (2009) proved that if a universal functional could be computed efficiently, QMA-complete problems could be solved efficiently (QMA is the quantum version of NP) [established]. So no formula works well for every system, and the only way forward is to exploit the structure of real chemistry. On data, Skala's performance comes from high-accuracy reference data "of unprecedented volume", and Meta's OMol25 used over 100 million DFT calculations [recent]. In strongly correlated systems (transition metals, FeMoco, cuprates) even CCSD(T) is unreliable, so there is no referee, and the problem is close to type B. Quantum computers are still far off: resource estimates for FeMoco have come down to about 1,500 logical qubits and about 10⁹ Toffoli gates (secondhand summary ⚠), still far beyond 2026 hardware; Lee et al. (2023) argue that there is no evidence of a generic exponential speedup for ground-state chemistry [established].

**What AI can do**
- *AI math/proof:* prove error bounds and exact constraints for approximations (e.g. the Lieb–Oxford bound), and design new algorithms with provable complexity. Useful, but only supporting: a theorem won't tell you the reaction barrier of a given catalyst.
- *Other AI capabilities (the main contributors):* neural network wave functions (FermiNet and others); neural network functionals (Skala has about 276,000 parameters and an error of 2.8 kcal/mol on GMTKN55, at the cost level of a semilocal functional; self-reported by the authors, independent replication pending ⚠); ML interatomic potentials (running molecular dynamics at "DFT accuracy and classical force field speed"); coding agents + active learning that automatically decide which expensive reference calculation to run next. With high-accuracy computation as an automatic referee, AI climbs fast.

**If solved: first-order impact** If "DFT cost + chemical accuracy" covers most of chemistry, transition metals included: computational screening of catalysts and battery materials becomes trustworthy, failed syntheses drop sharply, and reaction mechanisms can be computed before anyone runs them.

**If solved: second-order impact** Chemistry shifts to "simulate first, experiment later", like EDA in chip design. Quantum computing loses part of its flagship application [speculation]. Dual use: Urbina et al. (2022) reversed the objective of a drug-discovery model and generated about 40,000 candidate toxic molecules in 6 hours [established]. A lesson in specification gaming: Medvedev et al. (2017) found that over more than a decade, new functionals fit energies better and better while their densities drifted further and further from the exact solution [established].

**Timing** Optimistic: by end of 2027, ML functionals and potentials reach chemical accuracy for main-group and most transition-metal chemistry. Central: main-group chemistry is "solved" in a practical sense around 2028, while FeMoco-level strongly correlated systems are still disputed in 2030; this depends on **curve ①** (by mid-2027 agents can run for about 150 hours, enough to manage "data generation → training → evaluation" on their own) and has almost nothing to do with curve ②. Pessimistic: ML functionals fail to generalize to new elements and unusual bonding, and strongly correlated chemistry has to wait for fault-tolerant quantum computers after 2035.

**Counterarguments and uncertainty** Benchmark accuracy is not real catalysis: catalysis happens on surfaces, in solvents and under dynamic conditions, and is often an experimental question. ML functionals may silently go wrong outside their training distribution. For most drug projects, electronic-structure accuracy is not the rate-limiting step.

**Signals to watch** Skala-type functionals become the default option in mainstream codes and are independently replicated on transition metals; an ML-derived mechanism for FeMoco agrees with experiment. A signal in the other direction: quantum hardware beats the best classical methods on a chemistry problem for the first time (unlikely before 2030).

## 20.3 Problem 2: Inverse design and autonomous labs (batteries, catalysts, carbon capture)

**In one sentence** Turn "given a material, compute its properties" around into "given the properties you want, find the material and make it". AI can already generate millions of candidates cheaply, so the bottleneck has moved to synthesis, characterization, testing and scale-up, none of which are math problems.

**Background** The forward problem is "here is the recipe, taste the dish"; the inverse problem is "here is the taste you want, find the recipe, then actually cook it". Picking just 4 of about 80 commonly used elements gives C(80,4) = 1,581,580 combinations, which then have to be multiplied by ratios and crystal structures. Stability is judged with the **convex hull**: if AB has lower energy when it decomposes into A + B, then AB is unstable. But stable does not mean **synthesizable**; that depends on reaction pathways and kinetics, and there is no good theory for it.

*The real numbers in the funnel.* Generate (e.g. with the diffusion model MatterGen) → coarse screening with ML potentials → refined DFT → synthesis → characterization (X-ray diffraction, XRD) → performance testing → scale-up. GNoME (2023) predicted about 380,000 crystals on the convex hull; **736 of them, about 0.2%**, have been independently synthesized by outside groups [established]. A-Lab claimed to have synthesized 41 of 58 targets in 17 days; critics then pointed out that many of the "new materials" were ordered or disordered variants of known compounds [recent].

*By hand: Amdahl's law.* One discovery cycle = 2 weeks of design + 6 weeks of synthesis and testing. If AI cuts design to 0, the cycle goes from 8 weeks to 6, only 1.33× faster. To go 10× faster, the lab itself has to be 10× faster.

**Why it matters** Batteries determine how large a share renewables can take; catalysts are used for green ammonia and for producing hydrogen by water electrolysis (PEM electrolyzers depend on scarce iridium ⚠). The US Materials Genome Initiative (2011) said new materials typically take 10–20 years from discovery to market [established].

**What's blocking it: is math the bottleneck?** Basically not. The bottlenecks, in order: synthesizability (a type B chemistry problem); ambiguous characterization (the same XRD pattern can be read several ways); slow testing (battery lifetime tests take months); processing (performance depends on microstructure, defects and interfaces); data bias (failed experiments are almost never published). The math that is needed (Bayesian experimental design, symmetry enumeration) has long been mature.

**What AI can do**
- *AI math/proof:* direct effect close to zero.
- *Other AI capabilities (the main contributors):* generative models (MatterGen synthesized TaCr₂O₆ with a target bulk modulus of 200 GPa; measured about 169 GPa ⚠); coarse screening with ML potentials; LLMs extracting synthesis recipes from the literature; robotic closed-loop experiments and automated characterization. Periodic Labs (which raised a $300 million seed round in 2025) works on chip-cooling materials in the near term and superconductors in the long term [recent]. **The referee is experiment**, which is slow and noisy, so the ceiling on acceleration is set by lab throughput.

**If solved: first-order impact** If "half of predicted materials can be made, and their performance matches the prediction": R&D cycles shrink from 10–20 years to a few years, and batteries, catalysts and sorbents keep improving (less cobalt, lithium, iridium). More likely this looks like a few percent of improvement per year rather than a single leap [speculation].

**If solved: second-order impact** **Verification infrastructure becomes the new bottleneck**: we need "machine-checkable materials claims", with raw data, automated refinement results and reproducible synthesis records published together, the materials-science equivalent of Lean [speculation]. Replacing cobalt and rare earths with common elements would reshape supply chains. **AI safety**: autonomous labs are where AI first gets to act in the physical world, which raises chemical safety, dual use, and the question of "who can remotely order the synthesis of what".

**Timing** Optimistic: by 2028 autonomous lab throughput ×10, with a success rate of ≥50% on predicted targets. Central: in 2029–2032, AI-designed compositions enter niche commercial products; this depends on **curve ①** (by end of 2027 agents can work continuously for about 400 hours, enough to manage months of closed-loop experiments), but lab capital and physical cycle times are hard constraints, unrelated to curve ②. Pessimistic: synthesizability prediction stays poor, and together with a trust deficit this pushes things past 2035.

**Counterarguments and uncertainty** The gains may come mainly from optimizing known systems; models built around perfect crystals may focus on the wrong thing, because processing is what matters. Optimists argue that robots plus large models can raise throughput by two orders of magnitude and break the Amdahl constraint.

**Signals to watch** The fraction of AI-predicted materials reproduced by independent labs, and whether papers come with raw data; a battery maker mass-producing an AI-designed chemistry.

**Sources**: GNoME, Nature 624:80; A-Lab, Nature 624:86; A-Lab critique https://www.theregister.com/2024/01/31/ai_chemistry_research_disputed/; MatterGen arXiv:2312.03687; Periodic Labs https://siliconangle.com/2025/10/01/periodic-labs-raises-300m-accelerate-scientific-research-ai/

## 20.4 Problem 3: The mechanism of high-temperature superconductivity (the Hubbard model) and room-temperature superconductivity

**In one sentence** Cuprates conduct with zero resistance at about −140°C, and after 40 years there is still no accepted theory, so nobody can design a room-temperature superconductor "from a blueprint". Ironically, the highest-temperature superconductors (high-pressure hydrides) **don't rely on that theory at all**; they were computed with DFT.

**Background** Superconductivity = exactly zero resistance + expelling magnetic fields from the material. BCS theory (1957) explains conventional superconductivity: electrons pair up through lattice vibrations (phonons), like a person sinking into a trampoline and pulling the person next to them in. The ambient-pressure record for cuprates is about 133–138 K [established], and it is generally believed that the pairing comes from strong electron correlation and magnetism.

*The Hubbard model (two sites, by hand).* Electrons "hop" between lattice sites, lowering the energy by about t per hop; two electrons squeezed onto the same site pay a repulsion energy U. With 2 sites and 2 electrons, the ground-state energy is E = (U − √(U²+16t²))/2. At U = 0, E = −2t and the electrons are freely delocalized; at U = 8t, E ≈ −0.47t and the electrons are basically frozen on their own sites. The remaining gain comes from "virtual hopping when neighboring spins are antiparallel", which is equivalent to an antiferromagnetic coupling J ≈ 4t²/U = 0.5t. **Magnetism grows out of the competition between "hopping" and "repulsion"**, and the cuprate parent compounds are indeed antiferromagnets. The hard part: on an infinite 2D lattice with doping, is the ground state superconducting or a stripe state?

*This is a numerical dispute, not a dispute about proofs.* Qin et al. (2020): at t′ = 0, U = 8 there is no superconductivity; the ground state is a stripe state. Xu et al. (2024): once next-nearest-neighbor hopping t′ is added, superconductivity and stripes coexist. Roth et al. (2025-11, neural network quantum states): claim d-wave superconductivity even at U = 4, t′ = 0 [recent, unreviewed].

*Warnings and real progress.* In 2023 LK-99 was traced within weeks to an artifact caused by Cu₂S impurities [established]. The hydrides H₃S (203 K) and LaH₁₀ (about 250 K) superconduct under high pressures of about 150–170 GPa, and both were **predicted first by DFT and then confirmed by experiment** [established]. Strained nickelate thin films reached an onset Tc of about 26–42 K at ambient pressure in 2025 [recent]. The superconductors found by AI so far are all low-temperature: BEE-NET screened 1.3 million candidates down to 741, and experiments confirmed 2 [recent].

**Why it matters** Scientifically, it is the paradigm problem of strongly correlated electrons. Economically, ambient-pressure room-temperature superconductivity would mean lossless power transmission, cheaper MRI and more compact fusion magnets; but grid economics are mainly constrained by cable cost.

**What's blocking it: is math the bottleneck?** Mainly **type B + numerics**: nobody knows which model is right (one band or several? how big is t′? do phonons play a role?). The "sign problem" in fermionic quantum Monte Carlo is NP-hard in general (Troyer & Wiese 2005) [established], and this impossibility result pushes people toward tensor networks and neural network quantum states. Experimentally, sample quality is poor and high-pressure measurements are hard to reproduce. On the design side, there is no reliable Tc prediction for unconventional superconductors; for conventional ones there is (DFT + Eliashberg theory), and that is how the hydrides were found.

**What AI can do**
- *AI math/proof:* rigorously prove "superconductivity or not" in certain parameter regimes. This is far harder than numerics and does not directly produce materials; but the Haldane gap and Heisenberg ferromagnet results in Problem 7 show that simpler many-body models are now within range. Medium value: it can constrain theory.
- *Other AI capabilities (the main contributors):* neural network quantum states (already directly involved in the Hubbard dispute); ML-accelerated Tc screening; autonomous synthesis; using LLMs to clean up the literature and flag suspicious claims.

**If solved: first-order impact** Once the Hubbard phase diagram is settled, we will know whether a one-band model is enough and which parameter to tune. If ambient-pressure room-temperature superconductivity is discovered, it will set off a revolution in materials and engineering, but deployment will take decades.

**If solved: second-order impact** Power transmission, storage, motors, fusion magnets (Problem 5) and data-center power supply would all change. A nearer effect is epistemic: LK-99 showed the dynamics of "social media + replication race". When AI generates hundreds or thousands of candidate claims a day, the community will need a triage mechanism: standardized criteria (zero resistance, Meissner effect, specific heat, independent replication) plus public raw data [speculation].

**Timing** Consensus on the Hubbard phase diagram: optimistic 2027 (neural network quantum states and AFQMC converge), central 2028–2030, pessimistic: the debate turns into "which model to use for real materials"; this depends on compute and algorithms, not on curve ②. Ambient-pressure room-temperature superconductivity, independently verified: optimistic in the early 2030s (a tail event), central: not before 2035, pessimistic: it may not exist at all. AI may raise candidate throughput 2–5× [speculation] ⚠, but the hit rate is extremely low to begin with.

**Counterarguments and uncertainty** Understanding the mechanism is **neither necessary nor sufficient** for discovering new materials: the hydrides came from DFT, the nickelates from analogy plus luck. Neural network quantum states and AFQMC contradict each other, so even the "numerical facts" are not yet stable.

**Signals to watch** Different methods give a consistent phase diagram at the same parameters; nickelate ambient-pressure Tc above 77 K (the temperature of liquid nitrogen); an AI-designed superconductor with Tc > 30 K, independently replicated.

## 20.5 Problem 4: Turbulence (short card; for the Clay problem see §11)

The equations for water and air were written down in 1845, yet turbulence still has to be computed with empirical models. The Reynolds number (the ratio of inertia to viscosity) for an airliner wing is about 3×10⁷. The cost of directly computing every eddy (DNS) grows roughly as Re³, and at this Re the grid alone would need about 6×10¹⁶ points, so it has to be modeled: the effect of small eddies on the mean flow is filled in with empirical "closure models", which often fail on separated flow and stall (NASA's *CFD Vision 2030* lists this as one of the biggest bottlenecks [established]).

**On the engineering side the bottleneck is not math**, but scarce high-Re data, ML closures that fail as soon as the flow changes (Duraisamy et al. 2019), and aviation certification's requirement for verifiable models. The main contributors are AI's other capabilities: learned LES reaches the same accuracy on 2D turbulence with grids 8–10× coarser (Kochkov et al. 2021); coding agents port legacy code to GPUs, the most mundane item and possibly the one with the biggest effect on cost. Central judgment: not "basically solved" before 2030; more likely errors fall 10–30% per year (depends on compute, data and curve ①). **On the Clay side it is type A math**, and AI is already attacking it: OpenAI claims to have proved the forced branch, while the unforced branch is still open (§11); Chen–Hou used a computer-assisted proof to show that 3D Euler with a boundary blows up [established]; DeepMind and others found a family of unstable blowup solutions with accuracy that "meets the requirements of a computer-assisted proof" (2025-09) [recent]. Unforced branch: central 2028–2030 (curve ②; Millennium Problems will lag). A few theorems bear on turbulence physics: Isett proved the Onsager conjecture (energy can still be dissipated as viscosity goes to zero); Deng–Hani–Ma (2025) rigorously derived the NS–Fourier equations from hard-sphere particles [recent]. The two sides barely connect: engineering solvers never rely on global regularity. **Sources**: arXiv:2509.14185; Chen & Hou arXiv:2210.07191; Isett arXiv:1608.08301; Deng, Hani & Ma arXiv:2503.01800; Duraisamy et al. arXiv:1804.00183; Kochkov et al. arXiv:2102.01010

## 20.6 Problem 5: Fusion: stability, control and stellarator design

**In one sentence** The goal is to hold a plasma at over 100 million degrees in a magnetic field for long enough. Physical feasibility has been partly demonstrated; what remains is instability control, materials and engineering. Mathematical proof is only a small piece.

**Background** *How hot, and for how long (by hand).* Deuterium + tritium → helium + neutron + 17.6 MeV. Ignition roughly requires the Lawson criterion: density × temperature × confinement time ≳ 3×10²¹ keV·s/m³. With a density of 10²⁰ particles/m³ and a temperature of 15 keV (about 170 million degrees), the confinement time must be at least 3×10²¹ ÷ (10²⁰ × 15) = 2 seconds.

*The model ladder.* From exact to cheap: Vlasov–Maxwell (6-dimensional phase space, the subject of §12) → gyrokinetics (averages out the fast gyration around field lines; 5 dimensions) → magnetohydrodynamics (treats the plasma as a conducting fluid) → 1D transport codes (such as TORAX). The global smoothness result of §12 is a model-health proof at the top rung of this ladder, with no direct effect on engineering.

*Two kinds of magnetic cage.* A **tokamak** is axisymmetric and relies on a large current in the plasma itself for confinement; the price is that it can "disrupt": the current collapses within milliseconds, releasing huge forces and heat. A **stellarator** uses twisted 3D coils and can run in steady state; the price is that design becomes a 3D optimization problem.

*Grad's conjecture and its counterexample.* Good magnetic confinement needs the field lines to trace out layer upon layer of nested "magnetic surfaces", like an onion. Grad (1967) conjectured that without symmetry, no such smooth equilibrium exists; it has been called "the central mathematical problem of stellarator theory". On 2026-09-21, Gómez-Serrano, Liehr and Taylor gave a **counterexample**: a smooth equilibrium with only N-fold rotational symmetry, with a Lean 4 proof [recent, unreviewed]. The paper discloses the division of labor: humans wrote the roadmap, and GPT-5.6 Sol, Claude Fable 5 and Claude Opus 5 filled in the details and wrote the Lean. It is a model case of "**human-led + AI fills in details + formalization**". Physically it needs a discount: the field is exactly zero on the magnetic axis, and the result holds only in the limit of small curvature and large N. In practice engineers rely on numerical optimization (e.g. "precise quasisymmetry", Landreman & Paul 2022).

*Status.* In 2025-04 NIF produced 8.6 MJ from 2.08 MJ of laser energy (gain about 4.1), but the laser system drew about 300 MJ from the grid [recent]. ITER's deuterium-tritium operation has been pushed to 2039 ⚠; SPARC's first plasma has reportedly slipped to 2027 (secondhand ⚠). CFS and DeepMind (2025-10) are using TORAX to run millions of virtual experiments, combined with AlphaEvolve to search for operating scenarios, and plan to use reinforcement learning for real-time control later; Google also signed a 200 MW power purchase agreement for ARC [recent].

**Why it matters** Fusion is zero-carbon, stable baseload power, and the electricity demand of AI data centers is pushing tech companies toward fusion power purchase agreements. But in the end it has to compete on cost with "solar + storage" (Jassby 2017 ⚠).

**What's blocking it: is math the bottleneck?** Mainly **engineering and materials**: 14 MeV neutron damage to the first wall, tritium breeding ⚠, reliability of high-temperature superconducting magnets, divertor heat loads, cost. Next comes **physics**: predicting turbulent transport (a single multiscale simulation can take on the order of ten million CPU hours ⚠), disruptions and tearing modes. **Math is only a small part**: existence of 3D equilibria (Grad), model well-posedness (Vlasov–Maxwell with multiple particle species is still open), coil optimization.

**What AI can do**
- *AI math/proof:* Grad-type existence and rigidity theorems, rigorous stability criteria, equilibrium solvers with error bounds. They tell designers "which configurations exist", but they can't build a reactor.
- *Other AI capabilities (the main contributors):* reinforcement learning control (controlling 19 coils at once on TCV, Degrave et al. 2022; avoiding tearing instabilities in advance on DIII-D, Seo et al. 2024 [established]); disruption prediction; differentiable simulators and surrogate models for gyrokinetics; stellarator coil optimization; coding agents maintaining the simulation code stack.

**If solved: first-order impact** Faster device commissioning and higher performance; fewer disruptions, which is critical for ITER-class devices, where one major disruption can damage the machine; shorter stellarator design cycles.

**If solved: second-order impact** AI and energy form a positive feedback loop: fusion powers data centers, and AI speeds up fusion R&D [speculation]. **AI safety**: reinforcement learning controllers enter safety-critical physical systems; fusion is a near-ideal testbed for Guaranteed Safe AI (GSAI), since the physical models are good and the risks are physical but bounded, so one can do "finite-horizon certificates + runtime monitoring + backup controllers". **Nuclear proliferation**: fusion neutrons can breed fissile material, which needs safeguards oversight ⚠.

**Timing** SPARC reaching Q > 1 (fusion output power greater than heating power): optimistic 2027, central 2028, pessimistic after 2030. Commercial power: optimistic early 2030s, central mid-to-late 2030s, pessimistic in the 2040s or later. AI can shorten commissioning and design by months to years, but it can't change the real time needed to qualify materials under irradiation; progress is set by the hardware cadence, almost unrelated to curve ② and weakly related to curve ①.

**Counterarguments and uncertainty** AI control may just be icing on the cake: the physics of compact high-field tokamaks is already good enough, and the hard part is building them. Fusion may also never beat the cost curve of "solar + storage".

**Signals to watch** The actual dates of SPARC's first plasma and Q > 1; reinforcement learning controllers entering routine operation on large devices; Grad-type results being written into design codes.

## 20.7 Problem 6: Weather and climate (short card)

**Weather is an "initial value problem"**: know the atmosphere now, then integrate forward. Chaos is a hard constraint: suppose errors double every 2 days (an illustrative number); after 14 days they have grown 128×, so the limit of predictability is about two weeks [established]. AI models learn "what the atmosphere looks like 6 hours later" from more than 40 years of reanalysis data (ERA5): GraphCast (2023) beat ECMWF's operational model HRES on about 90% of targets, and GenCast (2024) beat ECMWF's ensemble forecast on about 97% of targets; **ECMWF's AIFS went operational on 2025-02-25**, cutting energy use by about 1,000× [recent]. The remaining bottlenecks are sparse observations, initial-condition assimilation and few samples of extreme events, none of which is math.

**Climate is a "boundary condition problem"**: what the statistical distribution of weather becomes after CO₂ doubles. IPCC AR6 gives a likely range of 2.5–4°C for equilibrium climate sensitivity, and the largest source of uncertainty is **cloud feedback**. This is a type B problem plus a data problem: there is no "doubled-CO₂ world" to train on, so AI can only extrapolate; the ACE2 authors themselves acknowledge that the model's sensitivity to changing sea surface temperature or CO₂ alone is "not entirely realistic" [recent]. End-to-end "observation → forecast" in operations: central 2028. Climate sensitivity range narrowed to about ±0.5°C: central in the mid-2030s, requiring kilometer-scale simulation plus AI cloud emulators. The same learned simulators have already rewritten operations for weather, which is scored every day, but move slowly on climate, which is scored once every few decades. This is the cleanest example of rule 3 in 20.10. **Sources**: GraphCast arXiv:2212.12794; GenCast arXiv:2312.15796; ACE2 arXiv:2411.11268; AIFS https://www.ecmwf.int/en/about/media-centre/news/2025/ecmwfs-ensemble-ai-forecasts-become-operational; IPCC AR6 WG1 Chapter 7

## 20.8 Problem 7: The Yang–Mills mass gap and making mathematical physics "rigorous"

**In one sentence** Physicists have made extremely precise predictions with quantum field theory, yet mathematicians still cannot prove that four-dimensional quantum Yang–Mills theory "exists" and has a mass gap. This is a Millennium Problem with a clear statement that AI might be the first to crack, but it has almost no effect on experiment or engineering; its value is conceptual and methodological.

**Background** *Quantum field theory (QFT).* The universe is full of "fields", and particles are ripples in those fields. QED's predictions agree with experiment to about the 10⁻¹² level ⚠, but the calculations use series that may diverge, and the infinities along the way are removed by "renormalization". **Mathematically, nobody has yet constructed an interacting 4D QFT that satisfies the axioms**; the most natural candidate, φ⁴₄, has been proved to reduce to a free theory in the continuum limit (Aizenman & Duminil-Copin 2021) [established].

*Yang–Mills (an analogy).* In electromagnetism light carries no charge, and two beams of light pass through each other. In Yang–Mills (such as QCD, which describes the strong force), the "light" (gluons) itself carries color charge and attracts itself, so it twists into flux tubes that lock quarks in. The **mass gap** says: in the equations gluons are massless, yet the lightest particle in the theory (the glueball) has positive mass. Lattice QCD puts the lightest glueball at about 1.7 GeV; about 99% of the mass of visible matter comes from QCD dynamics, not from the Higgs mechanism (Dürr et al. 2008) [established].

*What makes it hard.* The lattice method cuts spacetime into a grid with spacing a, computes a huge integral, then lets a → 0, like approximating an integral with Riemann sums. But here the integral is infinite-dimensional, over **all field configurations**, and an infinite-dimensional space has no ready-made "uniform measure"; to prove that the limit exists, is interacting and has a gap, you have to control fluctuations at all scales at once. 2D has been fully constructed, and there is progress on stochastic quantization in 2D and 3D (Chandra, Chevyrev, Hairer & Shen); 4D is still open as of 2026-10, and several "complete proofs" online are unreviewed ⚠.

*New in 2026: the type A list in mathematical physics is being cleared.* A secondhand count says this batch of OpenAI manuscripts contains about 25 "mathematical physics" families ⚠. The table below lists representatives found in the CONTENTS and lean/docs of `openai/math` (downloaded 2026-10-09); all are **claimed, unreviewed**:

| Family | Problem claimed solved (plain language) | Formalization |
|---|---|---|
| 261 | Anderson model (electrons hopping on a lattice with impurities): for d ≥ 3 and weak disorder, electrons can "spread out" and conduct (claims to solve this part of Simon's Problem 1) | Only the 2D part; 3D has **no Lean** |
| 271 | Spontaneous magnetization at low temperature of the d ≥ 3 quantum Heisenberg ferromagnet (quantum compass needles on a lattice; neighbors want to point the same way) | Has a Comparator statement |
| 268 | Haldane gap for spin-1 chains (the 1983 conjecture): the lowest excitation energy has a positive gap | None |

As of 2026-10-09 we found no named review by mathematical physicists. These results have one thing in common: what they confirm is something physicists already believed, such as that a 3D ferromagnet magnetizes at low temperature and a hard-sphere gas condenses; what §11 and §12 prove is that "the model does not break down on its own mathematically". These are **model-health proofs**: they answer "is the model logically healthy", not "what is nature like".

**Why it matters** Practical impact is about 0: lattice QCD already gave the numbers. Conceptual impact is large: is QFT a self-consistent mathematical theory? The methods will spill over into other branches of mathematics (precedent: Witten's TQFT gave knot invariants). It is also the best test case for whether AI can **build new theoretical frameworks**, not just solve problems.

**What's blocking it: is math the bottleneck?** Yes, but it is "type A statement, type B core": the statement is clear, but the proof may need new concepts (a rigorous 4D renormalization group, handling the Gribov ambiguity). There is also a formalization bottleneck [speculation]: physics has barely been formalized, HepLean/PhysLean only started in 2024, and Mathlib lacks infrastructure such as functional integration. Just writing the **statement** "4D Yang–Mills satisfies the Osterwalder–Schrader axioms" in Lean is a huge specification project, the pure form of "the bottleneck moves to the statement" (§3, §18).

**What AI can do**
- *AI math/proof (the main contributor):* proofs in constructive QFT are known for extremely long, "bookkeeping" estimates (cluster expansions, multiscale analysis), which is exactly the kind of labor AI is best at; the 2026 batch of results shows that problems in the Lieb and Simon tradition are now within range.
- *Other AI capabilities:* flow-model sampling for lattice QCD (Albergo et al. 2019); formalizing physics; symbolic computation.

**If solved: first-order impact** The first rigorous interacting 4D QFT is born; large parts of the Lieb and Simon lists get closed, and mathematical physics textbooks have to be rewritten.

**If solved: second-order impact** The field's center of gravity shifts from "proving" to "choosing and auditing statements", and there is a low-probability "rigor dividend": finding that some of physicists' default assumptions don't hold. A rigorous renormalization group connects to deep learning theory (20.10.2). "A Millennium Problem with physical meaning solved by AI" would cause a jump in public perception and would also test verification institutions: Clay requires a two-year period of community acceptance, while AI produces results much faster; statement faithfulness is still an issue (the lesson of forced NS, §11).

**Timing** Lieb/Simon-type many-body problems: the central judgment is that by end of 2027, most well-known type A problems in mathematical physics will be claimed solved, with more than half confirmed by experts [speculation]; the basis is that a batch already appeared in 2026-09, and "famous but relatively tractable" problems will fall ahead of curve ②'s average (about 18% by end of 2027). Yang–Mills mass gap: optimistic 2028, central 2030–2033, pessimistic after 2035 [speculation]; curve ②'s 77% in 2030 refers to typical problems, and Millennium-level ones will lag; curve ③ makes proofs cheaper, but the specification cost of the statement does not fall.

**Counterarguments and uncertainty** The effective field theory view holds that the Standard Model is only valid below some energy scale, so rigorously constructing the continuum limit means studying an idealized object. A rigorous proof may only confirm what the lattice already "saw". AI proofs may be "unreadable by humans" (Gómez-Serrano commenting on the NS paper), which discounts their conceptual value.

**Signals to watch** 271, 261 and 268 get confirmation from named experts or are published in journals; statements of the QFT axioms appear in Mathlib or PhysLean; Clay takes a position.

## 20.9 Problem 8: Quantum gravity and string theory (short card)

General relativity says gravity is smooth curvature of spacetime; quantum mechanics is discrete and probabilistic; forcing the two together produces infinities that can't be removed. Candidate theories (string theory, loop quantum gravity and others) are mathematically very rich but make almost no testable predictions. The reason can be worked out by hand: the energy scale where quantum gravity becomes significant is about 1.2×10¹⁹ GeV, while the LHC reaches only 1.36×10⁴ GeV, a gap of about 10¹⁵; at fixed magnetic field, energy is proportional to ring radius, so scaling the LHC's radius of about 4.3 km up by 10¹⁵ gives about 400 light-years. The number of ways string theory's extra dimensions can be compactified is estimated at 10⁵⁰⁰ to 10^272,000 (Taylor & Wang 2015), which makes a unique prediction hard.

**The first bottleneck is experiment; the second is type B math** (string theory has no complete non-perturbative definition), but type A subproblems are everywhere: scattering amplitude identities, bootstrap bounds, Calabi–Yau classification. Using only symmetry, self-consistency and semidefinite programming, the conformal bootstrap computed the 3D Ising critical exponents to 5–6 decimal places (Kos et al. 2016) [established]. AI has already entered: in 2026-02, Guevara, Strominger and others (for OpenAI) showed that the "single-minus-helicity gluon tree amplitude", long assumed to be zero, is nonzero in particular configurations; the formula was proposed by GPT-5.2 Pro, proved by an internal model and verified by the authors [recent]. Results like this will ramp up in 2027 (curve ②); as for experimental confirmation of quantum gravity, the central judgment is not before 2040. AI's most valuable contribution may be designing tabletop experiments, for example testing whether gravity can produce entanglement (Bose et al. 2017, not yet realized) [speculation]. Conversely, a field with no experimental constraints, plus AI producing unlimited **self-consistent** mathematics, could lead to a flood of beautiful theories with no selection pressure: Lean can verify the proof, but not "this is how nature is" [speculation]. **Sources**: arXiv:2602.12176; Taylor & Wang arXiv:1511.03209; Kos et al. arXiv:1603.04436; Bose et al. arXiv:1707.06050

## 20.10 Second-order effects in this field

### 20.10.1 Three rules

**Rule 1: most "math breakthroughs" in physics are model-health proofs.** NS (§11), Vlasov–Maxwell (§12), Anderson, Heisenberg, BEC and Yang–Mills all confirm things physicists already believe. The math that really changes direction comes in three kinds: **impossibility theorems** (Schuch–Verstraete, Troyer–Wiese, which push a field toward approximations that "exploit structure"); **existence results and counterexamples** (the Grad counterexample, the Onsager conjecture, which tell you what is in the design space); **new algorithms**. So AI math's biggest value to physics may lie in designing algorithms and proving impossibility, not in sweeping up conjectures.

**Rule 2: candidates are cheap, verification is expensive.** In math it is "proofs are cheap, auditing statements is expensive" (§18); in physics and chemistry it is "candidates are cheap, verification is expensive", where verification relies on experiment, high-accuracy computation, or even time (climate). GNoME's 380,000 versus 736, AI superconductor candidates plus LK-99-style frenzies, and a single release of 719 manuscripts with almost no named reviewers all have the same shape. The next scarce resource is **verification infrastructure**: autonomous replication labs, public raw data, statement auditing.

**Rule 3: how fast AI speeds up a field is set by the speed of its referee.**

| Field | Referee | One feedback round | AI acceleration |
|---|---|---|---|
| Weather | Tomorrow's observations | A day | Already happened |
| Mathematical physics theorems | Lean | Minutes to hours | Already happened (2026-09) |
| Electronic structure | High-accuracy computation | Hours to days | Fast |
| Fusion control | Simulator + plasma shots | Seconds to months | Medium |
| Materials and superconductors | Synthesis + characterization | Weeks to months | Limited by Amdahl |
| Climate | Observation | A decade | Slow |
| Quantum gravity | None | ∞ | Only on subproblems |

This generalizes type A / type B (§18): from "is there a referee" to "how fast is the referee".

### 20.10.2 Cross-field connections

1. **Spin glasses → neural networks → protein folding.** The energy function of the Hopfield network (1982) is the Hamiltonian of a spin glass, and memories are energy minima (2024 Nobel Prize in Physics). Bryngelson & Wolynes (1987) used the same theory to explain the "energy funnel" of protein folding. The Mézard–Parisi result of §13, OpenAI family 281 (QAOA reaches the optimum on the SK model) and the random k-SAT threshold all use the same mathematics; so do attractor networks for working memory in neuroscience.
2. **Renormalization group ↔ deep learning.** The renormalization group (RG) coarse-grains details layer by layer and looks at which quantities survive as the scale grows. Mehta & Schwab mapped RG onto restricted Boltzmann machines; Roberts, Yaida & Hanin wrote layer-by-layer representations as an RG flow; Halverson, Maiti & Stoner pointed out that infinitely wide networks are free fields and that finite width brings interactions. If AI produces a breakthrough in rigorous RG (Problem 7), deep learning theory may benefit directly; the free-energy and phase-transition language of singular learning theory (SLT) is statistical physics to begin with (§22) [speculation].
3. **Non-equilibrium thermodynamics → diffusion models → materials generation.** Diffusion models (Sohl-Dickstein et al. 2015) come from the physical picture of "add noise until equilibrium, then learn the reverse process", and MatterGen now brings them back to materials.
4. **"Simulators fail out of distribution" is a shared motif.** Weather models pushed to climate, ML functionals pushed to new elements, turbulence closures pushed to new flows, fusion surrogate models pushed to new devices. This is isomorphic to distribution shift and goal misgeneralization in alignment, and since physics has ground truth, it is a clean testbed for studying them [speculation].
5. **Complexity theory is the seam between physics and CS.** QMA (DFT), NP-hardness (the sign problem) and the evidence on quantum advantage (Lee et al.) together determine "which problems can only ever be approximated"; AI math output along this seam (OpenAI families 279 and 284, see §19) may have more impact than purely physical results [speculation].

### 20.10.3 For society and AI safety

- **The physical sciences will be where autonomous AI research gets going first**, and capability spillover will show up first at the physical execution end (autonomous labs, device control, simulation-driven design), forming a compute feedback loop (fusion power purchase agreements, chip-cooling materials).
- **Dual use**: chemistry is the most direct (toxins, energetic materials), followed by nuclear and plasma. Cheap climate simulators also lower the barrier to simulating unilateral stratospheric injection (geoengineering), which is exactly the out-of-distribution regime where simulators tend to be "confident but wrong" [speculation].
- **Epistemics**: the volume of claims grows by orders of magnitude while review capacity stays the same. Experimental science needs its own "Lean": autonomous replication plus raw data.
- **Specification-gaming examples**: forced NS, DFT density drift, and early GraphCast "smoothing out" extreme events because it optimized mean squared error.
- **A real-world testbed for GSAI**: fusion control, chemical processes and power grids all have good physical models and suit "bounded certificates + runtime monitoring".
